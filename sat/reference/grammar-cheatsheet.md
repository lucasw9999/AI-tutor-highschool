# SAT Grammar Rules Cheat-Sheet

**Why this matters for your score:** Standard English Conventions is ~26% of your Reading & Writing section (11-15 questions). These are the *most learnable* points on the whole SAT — every question follows the exact same prompt ("Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?"), and there are only about a dozen rules. Memorize this page and you can answer most SEC questions in under 20 seconds, banking time for the hard reading. Easy points. Take them all.

---

## THE 20-SECOND METHOD (do this on every SEC question)

1. **Read the four choices FIRST.** What's changing tells you the rule.
   - Punctuation changing (`,` `;` `:` `—`)? → It's a **Boundaries / sentence-joining** question.
   - Verbs changing (*is/are*, *was/were*, *has/have*)? → **Subject-verb agreement** or **verb tense**.
   - Pronouns changing (*it/they*, *who/whom*, *its/their*)? → **Pronoun agreement / case**.
   - Word order or `-ing`/`-ed` opener? → **Modifier placement** (dangling modifier).
   - List or paired structure? → **Parallelism**.
2. **Find the subject and the verb** of the sentence. Most rules key off this.
3. **Shortest correct answer usually wins** — the SAT rewards "no unnecessary punctuation." When in doubt between equal options, pick less punctuation.

## PART 1 — BOUNDARIES (punctuation + joining clauses)

> **Core idea:** An *independent clause* = a complete sentence (subject + verb, can stand alone). A *dependent clause* cannot stand alone (*because I left, which she saw*). The #1 tested skill is **how you may legally join two independent clauses.**

### The 5 legal ways to join two independent clauses
| # | Method | Example |
|---|--------|---------|
| 1 | **Period** | *The rain stopped. We left.* |
| 2 | **Semicolon** `;` | *The rain stopped; we left.* |
| 3 | **Comma + FANBOYS** | *The rain stopped, so we left.* |
| 4 | **Colon** `:` (2nd part explains 1st) | *We knew one thing: the rain had stopped.* |
| 5 | **Make one dependent** | *Because the rain stopped, we left.* |

**FANBOYS** = **F**or **A**nd **N**or **B**ut **O**r **Yet **S**o (the only coordinating conjunctions).

### The two errors these prevent
- **Comma splice** (most-tested error): two complete sentences joined by *only a comma*. → WRONG. *The rain stopped, we left.* ✗
- **Run-on / fused**: two complete sentences with *no* punctuation. → WRONG. *The rain stopped we left.* ✗

### Semicolon vs. Colon vs. Dash — decision tree
- **Semicolon `;`** → use ONLY between two complete sentences (test: can you swap it for a period? If yes, `;` is legal). Also separates list items that already contain commas.
- **Colon `:`** → the part *before* the colon must be a complete sentence; what follows explains, lists, or defines it. *She had one goal: to win.*
- **Dash `—`** → flexible. Can replace a colon (introduce/explain) or be used in a *pair* to set off an interruption (like commas or parentheses). *The team — exhausted but proud — finished.*
- **MATCHING RULE:** an interrupter must be bracketed by the *same* punctuation on both sides. *My brother, a doctor, lives here.* ✓ — never *My brother, a doctor — lives here.* ✗

### Commas — when you DO and DON'T use them
| Use a comma | Don't use a comma |
|---|---|
| After an intro phrase: *After lunch, we left.* | Between subject and its verb: *The dog ~~,~~ barked.* ✗ (the **Q14 rule**) |
| Before FANBOYS joining 2 sentences | Before a verb's object/complement |
| Around **non-essential** info: *My car, which is red, broke down.* | Around **essential** info: *The car that I bought broke down.* (no commas) |
| In a list of 3+: *red, white, and blue* | Between two items joined by *and*: *I ran ~~,~~ and jumped.* ✗ |

**Essential vs. non-essential (the *which* vs. *that* rule):**
- Non-essential = remove it, sentence still makes sense → **set off with commas** (or dashes/parens). Use *which*.
- Essential = needed to identify the noun → **no commas**. Use *that*.

### Apostrophes (tested as "which choice" with `its/it's`, `their/they're`)
- **Possessive singular:** *the dog's bone.* **Possessive plural:** *the dogs' bones.*
- **its** = belonging to it. **it's** = it is. (No apostrophe in the possessive!)
- **their** = possessive. **they're** = they are. **there** = place.
- **whose** = possessive. **who's** = who is.
- Plurals get NO apostrophe: *the 1990s*, *three cats* (not *cat's*).

## PART 2 — FORM, STRUCTURE, AND SENSE (grammar of words/phrases)

### Subject-Verb Agreement
- Singular subject → singular verb; plural → plural. *The student **is**; the students **are**.*
- **Ignore the prepositional phrase** between subject and verb — the SAT hides the real subject. *The box of chocolates **is** (not are) empty.* (subject = box, not chocolates)
- **Collective nouns** (team, group, jury, family) = singular. *The team **wins**.*
- *Each, every, everyone, anyone, neither, either, none, nobody* = **singular**. *Each of the players **is** ready.*
- "There is/are" → verb agrees with what comes *after*. *There **are** two reasons.*

### Pronoun Agreement & Case
- A pronoun must match its noun in number. Singular noun → singular pronoun. *A student must bring **his or her** (not their) book* — though SAT now accepts singular *they* in some contexts, **match number** is the safe default; watch for singular noun + *they/their* mismatches.
- **its/their** must match: *The company raised **its** (not their) prices.*
- **Case:** **who** = subject (he/she); **whom** = object (him/her). Test: answer with *he* → who; *him* → whom.
- **Pronoun-antecedent must be unambiguous** — if it's unclear what *it/this/they* refers to, that's the error.

### Verb Tense & Form
- Keep tense **consistent** with the rest of the passage unless time-words signal a shift (*yesterday, by 2020, currently*).
- **Past perfect** (*had + verb*) = the earlier of two past actions. *By the time we arrived, the show **had ended**.*
- Watch irregular forms: *had **gone*** (not *went*), *had **seen*** (not *saw*).

### Modifiers (the Q15 rule — dangling/misplaced modifier)
- **An opening modifier phrase must be immediately followed by the noun it describes.**
  - ✓ *Walking home, **Lucas** saw a fox.* (Lucas was walking)
  - ✗ *Walking home, **a fox** appeared.* (the fox wasn't walking — dangling)
- When a sentence opens with an `-ing` or `-ed` phrase + comma, the **very next word** must be the do-er. This is a top-tested trap — check it every time you see a comma-opener.

### Parallelism
- Items in a list or comparison must share the same grammatical form.
  - ✓ *I like **running**, **swimming**, and **biking**.* (all -ing)
  - ✗ *I like **running**, **swimming**, and **to bike**.*
- Paired conjunctions stay parallel: *not only X **but also** Y*; *either X **or** Y*.

### Logical Comparison (often grouped here)
- Compare like things. *Her novel is longer than **Smith's** (not than Smith).* — compare novel to novel, not novel to a person.

---

## NOT grammar — don't confuse these (Transitions = Expression of Ideas)
Words like *however, therefore, for example, in fact, meanwhile, nevertheless* test **logic**, not punctuation. Strategy: cover the transition, read both sentences, decide the relationship (contrast? cause? example? addition?), THEN pick the word. Different skill, different brain mode.

## PRACTICE SET (12 questions — same format as the real test)

*Prompt for all: "Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?"*

1. The museum's new wing opened last spring ______ it has already drawn record crowds.
   A) `spring,` B) `spring;` C) `spring` D) `spring:`

2. The flock of geese ______ over the lake every morning at dawn.
   A) `fly` B) `flies` C) `are flying` D) `have flown`

3. ______ from the summit, the entire valley looked impossibly small.
   A) `Viewing` B) `Viewed` C) `To view` D) `When we viewed it`

4. My oldest sister, a marine biologist ______ studies coral reefs off the coast of Belize.
   A) `, who` B) `who` C) `—` D) `,`

5. Each of the three proposals ______ a different solution to the budget shortfall.
   A) `offer` B) `offering` C) `offers` D) `have offered`

6. The committee announced ______ decision on Friday, surprising everyone.
   A) `it's` B) `their` C) `its` D) `there`

7. The hikers wanted to reach camp before dark; ______ they quickened their pace.
   A) `therefore,` B) `however,` C) `for example,` D) `nevertheless,`

8. The recipe calls for flour, sugar ______ and two eggs.
   A) `,` B) `;` C) (no punctuation) D) `:`

9. By the time the firefighters arrived, the blaze ______ most of the barn.
   A) `consumed` B) `had consumed` C) `consumes` D) `has consumed`

10. The scientist whose research won the award ______ presenting tomorrow.
    A) `are` B) `is` C) `were` D) `be`

11. To stay healthy, the doctor recommends eating well, sleeping enough ______
    A) `, and exercise regularly.` B) `, and to exercise regularly.` C) `, and exercising regularly.` D) `; and regular exercise.`

12. The novel explores one central theme ______ the cost of ambition.
    A) `,` B) `;` C) `:` D) (no punctuation)

---

## WORKED ANSWERS

1. **B** (`spring;`). Two complete sentences ("The museum's new wing opened last spring" / "it has already drawn record crowds"). A comma = comma splice (✗); a colon doesn't fit (2nd isn't an explanation of the 1st); semicolon is the legal join. *Rule: 5 ways to join clauses.*

2. **B** (`flies`). Subject = *flock* (singular collective noun), not *geese*. Ignore the prepositional phrase "of geese." A singular subject needs *flies*. *Rule: subject-verb agreement + ignore the middle phrase.*

3. **B** (`Viewed`). *Viewed from the summit, the entire valley looked impossibly small* — the past-participial opener correctly modifies the noun right after the comma (*the entire valley*, which is what's viewed), and it's the concise, SAT-preferred phrasing. A (*Viewing*) makes the valley do the viewing (dangling); C (*To view*) dangles; D (*When we viewed it*) is grammatical but wordier and adds the redundant pronoun *it*. *Rule: a participial opener must modify the noun directly after the comma — and prefer the concise, non-dangling option.*

4. **D** (`,`). The appositive *a marine biologist* is non-essential and must be **closed with a comma** (matching the comma after *sister*), leaving *studies* as the sentence's main verb: *My oldest sister, a marine biologist, studies coral reefs off the coast of Belize.* A (`, who`) turns the rest into a subordinate *who*-clause, so the sentence is left with **no main verb → a fragment**; B (`who`, no comma) also fragments it; C (dash) mismatches the opening comma. *Rule: close a non-essential appositive with matching punctuation, and every sentence needs a main verb.*

5. **C** (`offers`). Subject = *Each* (always singular). Ignore "of the three proposals." *Rule: each/every/either = singular.*

6. **C** (`its`). The committee (singular) possesses the decision → possessive *its* (no apostrophe). *it's* = "it is" (✗); *their* mismatches the singular subject; *there* = place. *Rule: its vs. it's + pronoun agreement.*

7. **A** (`therefore,`). Logic = cause/effect (wanted to arrive → so they sped up). *Therefore* fits; *however/nevertheless* signal contrast; *for example* signals an example. *(Transition question — logic, not grammar.)*

8. **A** (comma) — *flour, sugar, and two eggs.* The SAT uses the **serial (Oxford) comma**: in a list of three or more items, put a comma between each item *including* before the final *and*. (Same rule as the list example above — *red, white, and blue.*) **B** (semicolon) and **D** (colon) are wrong — those join or introduce complete clauses, not items in a simple list; and **C** (no comma) leaves the three-item list without its separating comma.

   *(Teaching point: on the real SAT the "obviously wrong heavy punctuation" choices — the colon and semicolon here — let you eliminate fast. Between a bare comma and nothing, pick the one that doesn't break a rule.)*

9. **B** (`had consumed`). Two past events; the consuming happened *before* the firefighters arrived → past perfect *had consumed*. *Rule: past perfect = the earlier past action.*

10. **B** (`is`). Subject = *scientist* (singular). "whose research won the award" is a modifying clause to ignore. *Rule: subject-verb agreement; ignore the interrupting clause.*

11. **C** (`, and exercising regularly.`). Parallel structure: *eating, sleeping, exercising* (all -ing). B breaks it (*to exercise*); A breaks it (*exercise*); D changes form and adds a wrong semicolon. *Rule: parallelism.*

12. **C** (`:`). The first part ("The novel explores one central theme") is a complete sentence; what follows *defines* the theme. A colon introduces a definition/explanation. A comma is too weak; a semicolon needs a complete sentence after it ("the cost of ambition" is a fragment); no punctuation runs them together. *Rule: colon introduces an explanation after a complete clause.*
