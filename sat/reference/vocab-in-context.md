# Words-in-Context Method + High-Frequency Words

**Why this matters for your score:** Words in Context is the single most-tested Reading & Writing skill (~6–8 of 54 questions, part of the ~28% Craft & Structure domain). The SAT does NOT test memorized definitions — it tests whether you can read a word's meaning *from the sentence around it*. Master the method below and you bank an easy 6–8 points; the word list just makes the method faster.

---

## The one rule: the passage defines the word, not the dictionary

Every Words-in-Context question gives you a short passage (25–150 words) with one blank or one underlined word. The correct answer is whatever **the surrounding sentences force** the word to mean — even if another choice is a "more common" definition of that word. The test loves words with multiple meanings precisely to punish people who pick the familiar meaning instead of the contextual one.

---

## The 4-step COVER method (do this on EVERY question)

1. **COVER** the blank / underlined word and the four answer choices. Don't read them yet — they're designed to bias you.
2. **PREDICT** your own word or phrase from context. Read the whole short passage. Ask: *what would I write here?* Even a clumsy phrase ("makes-bigger," "not-sure") works.
3. **MATCH** your prediction to the closest answer choice. Now uncover the four options.
4. **CHECK** by plugging the winner back into the sentence — and confirm it doesn't contradict any other sentence. If two answers seem to fit, the right one fits the *tone and logic of the whole passage*, not just the one sentence.

**Look for context clues:**
- **Contrast signals** ("but," "however," "although," "unlike," "despite") → the word means the OPPOSITE of the nearby idea.
- **Continuation signals** ("and," "similarly," "because," "in fact," ";") → the word means the SAME as the nearby idea.
- **Definition/restatement** ("that is," dashes, commas, "in other words") → the sentence basically hands you the meaning.
- **Tone** (positive vs. negative): even if you can't pin the exact meaning, you can often eliminate 2 choices by charge alone.

---

## Worked example (see the method run)

> Although the committee had expected the new policy to be met with resistance, the response was surprisingly ______: nearly every member voted to adopt it without debate.

- **Cover & predict:** "Although...resistance" + "nearly every member voted...without debate" → contrast signal. They expected pushback; instead everyone agreed easily. Predict: *agreeable / accepting*.
- **Match the choices:** (A) hostile (B) favorable (C) confusing (D) delayed → **(B) favorable** matches "accepting."
- **Check:** "the response was surprisingly favorable" fits "voted to adopt it without debate." (A) is the trap — it matches "resistance," ignoring the "Although" reversal.

That "Although" did all the work. **Signal words decide most of these questions.**

---

## The 4 trap types (College Board's actual wrong-answer logic)

| Trap | What it looks like | How to beat it |
|---|---|---|
| **Familiar-meaning trap** | A common definition of a multi-meaning word that doesn't fit *this* sentence (e.g., "novel" = book, when context needs "new") | Always predict from context FIRST, before seeing choices |
| **Right charge, wrong precision** | Correct positive/negative tone but too strong or too weak | Re-plug and compare degree to the passage |
| **Opposite (reversal)** | The exact opposite meaning — works only if you missed a contrast word | Circle every "but / although / however" before answering |
| **Sounds smart, off-topic** | A hard, impressive word that doesn't match the sentence's logic | Hard ≠ correct. Match meaning, not difficulty |

## High-frequency SAT words & word-types (the ones that actually recur)

These appear repeatedly on the digital SAT. Learn them as **flexible meanings**, not rigid definitions — the test bends them to context.

**Tier 1 — "academic verbs" (most common; describe what authors/texts DO):**
| Word | Quick meaning |
|---|---|
| **convey / illustrate** | show, communicate an idea |
| **undermine** | weaken, work against |
| **bolster / reinforce** | strengthen, support |
| **underscore / emphasize** | stress, highlight |
| **qualify** | limit or soften a claim |
| **reconcile** | make two things fit together |
| **anticipate** | expect / address in advance |
| **prompt** | cause, trigger |

**Tier 2 — multi-meaning "trap" words (the test exploits the less-common sense):**
| Word | Common sense → SAT sense |
|---|---|
| **novel** | a book → new, original |
| **qualify** | pass a test → limit/restrict |
| **arrest** | to detain → to stop/halt (e.g., "arrest decline") |
| **sound** | noise → valid, reliable ("sound argument") |
| **economy** | money system → efficient use ("economy of words") |
| **discriminate** | be biased → distinguish/tell apart |
| **reservation** | a booking → doubt/hesitation |

**Tier 3 — high-utility adjectives:**
| Word | Quick meaning |
|---|---|
| **nuanced** | subtle, with fine distinctions |
| **ambivalent** | having mixed feelings |
| **skeptical** | doubting |
| **plausible** | believable |
| **tentative** | uncertain, not final |
| **comprehensive** | complete, covering everything |
| **ubiquitous** | found everywhere |
| **arbitrary** | based on whim, not reason |
| **superficial** | shallow, surface-level |
| **pragmatic** | practical |
| **inevitable** | unavoidable |
| **redundant** | repetitive, unnecessary |

**Tier 4 — tone words (decide positive vs. negative fast):**
- Positive: **astute, prudent, candid, meticulous, resilient, robust**
- Negative: **contentious, dubious, austere, superfluous, derivative, tenuous**
- Neutral/analytical: **objective, empirical, theoretical, hypothetical, methodical**

> Tip: You don't need a 2,000-word list. Knowing ~150 of these high-utility words + the COVER method beats memorizing 1,000 obscure words you'll never see.

## Practice set (10 questions)

*Cover the choices, predict, then match. Answers + reasoning below.*

> **On the real SAT the correct letter is random** — there is no "when in doubt pick B." The answers below are deliberately spread across all four letters, so the only way through is the method.

**1.** While most critics praised the film's bold visuals, they were ______ about its thin plot, noting it left key questions unanswered.
(A) enthusiastic (B) indifferent (C) silent (D) skeptical

**2.** The researcher's conclusions were widely respected because her methods were ______: every step was documented and could be repeated by others.
(A) arbitrary (B) innovative (C) rigorous (D) controversial

**3.** Far from being ______, the senator's apology was carefully worded to avoid admitting any actual wrongdoing.
(A) candid (B) lengthy (C) public (D) emotional

**4.** Although the new evidence did not completely overturn the old theory, it did ______ several of its central claims.
(A) undermine (B) reinforce (C) restate (D) clarify

**5.** The essay's argument is ______; rather than offering one simple cause, it weighs several interacting factors.
(A) flawed (B) nuanced (C) brief (D) biased

**6.** The author uses the anecdote to ______ her main point, giving readers a concrete example of an otherwise abstract idea.
(A) contradict (B) complicate (C) question (D) illustrate

**7.** Critics called the sequel ______, complaining it merely recycled ideas from the original without adding anything new.
(A) derivative (B) innovative (C) polarizing (D) obscure

**8.** Engineers added the backup system not because failure was likely but because, over decades of use, some breakdown was effectively ______.
(A) preventable (B) catastrophic (C) trivial (D) inevitable

**9.** Her support for the proposal was ______; she agreed to vote yes but reserved the right to withdraw if costs rose.
(A) wholehearted (B) tentative (C) hostile (D) immediate

**10.** The committee adopted a ______ approach, focusing on what would actually work rather than on lofty ideals.
(A) theoretical (B) visionary (C) pragmatic (D) reluctant

---

## Answers (worked)

**1. (D) skeptical.** Contrast signal "While…praised" sets up an opposite; "thin plot…questions unanswered" is doubt. (A) enthusiastic reverses it; (B) indifferent fails because they cared enough to *note* the flaw; (C) silent flatly contradicts "noting." *Trap beaten: reversal.*

**2. (C) rigorous.** The colon restates the meaning — "every step documented and repeatable" = thorough, careful. (A) arbitrary is the opposite charge; (B) innovative is praise, but the sentence praises *carefulness*, not originality; (D) controversial contradicts "widely respected." *Clue: restatement after the colon.*

**3. (A) candid.** "Far from being ___" + "avoid admitting wrongdoing" → the blank is the opposite of evasive = honest, frank. (B)/(C)/(D) are true-sounding but ignore the logic. *Trap beaten: off-topic but plausible.*

**4. (A) undermine.** "Although…did not completely overturn…it did ___" → the contrast promises the evidence still did *damage*. (B) reinforce reverses it; (C) restate and (D) clarify are both harmless, so they waste the "did." *Academic verb you must know.*

**5. (B) nuanced.** "rather than one simple cause, it weighs several interacting factors" defines the word: subtle, multi-sided. *Definition handed to you in the sentence.*

**6. (D) illustrate.** "giving readers a concrete example of an abstract idea" = to show, to demonstrate. (A) contradict and (C) question carry the wrong (negative) function; (B) complicate is the opposite of what a clarifying example does. *Core academic verb.*

**7. (A) derivative.** "merely recycled ideas…without adding anything new" defines it: unoriginal. (B) innovative is the exact opposite; (C) polarizing and (D) obscure describe how people *reacted* or how *visible* it was, not whether it was original. *Trap beaten: (B) feels like praise, but the tone here is a complaint.*

**8. (D) inevitable.** "not because failure was likely but because…over decades of use" → given enough time, unavoidable. (A) preventable reverses it; (B) catastrophic is about *how bad* a breakdown is, but the sentence is about *how certain* it is; (C) trivial misses why you'd bother with a backup. *Contrast + tone.*

**9. (B) tentative.** "agreed…but reserved the right to withdraw" → conditional, not firm. (A) wholehearted reverses it. *Clue: "but…reserved the right."*

**10. (C) pragmatic.** "what would actually work rather than lofty ideals" defines practical. (A) theoretical and (B) visionary both side with the "lofty ideals" the sentence explicitly rejects. *Definition + contrast.*

---

**If you missed any:** the fix is almost always the same — you read the choices before predicting, or you skipped a contrast word. Re-cover, re-predict. The sentence always tells you the answer.
