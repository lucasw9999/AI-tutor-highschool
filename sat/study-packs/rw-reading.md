# Reading & Writing — Information & Ideas + Craft & Structure

**Why this matters for your score:** These two domains are ~54% of your Reading & Writing section (~25–29 of 54 questions). They're where the nastiest hard-Module-2 traps hide, so cleaning them up is the single biggest lever to push RW from "okay" toward a 750+ — your half of a 1500.

---

## How to use this (read once, then just do questions)

- **You learn by missing.** Do a question, check it, and if you got it wrong, *re-read the trap rule below it before moving on.* That's the whole method.
- **The format you're training for:** each question has its own tiny passage (25–150 words) + one 4-choice question. No long shared passages. No calculator (RW). ~71 seconds per question average. Digital, in Bluebook, multistage adaptive — Module 1 accuracy sets your ceiling, so M1 precision is everything.
- **Order on the real test:** within a module, questions are grouped by skill and run **easy → hard**. When difficulty suddenly drops, you've hit a new skill cluster — recalibrate.

### The universal 5-step loop (use on EVERY question)
1. **Read the whole blurb first.** It's short. Don't skim.
2. **Read the question stem and identify the TASK** (main idea? detail? support? infer? word? structure?). The verb in the stem decides everything.
3. **Predict the answer in your own words** before looking at choices.
4. **Eliminate against the text, not your opinion.** If the text doesn't say it, it's wrong — even if it's true in real life.
5. **Pick the choice the text forces.** The right answer is the one with textual warrant, not the one that "sounds smart."

### The 6 trap families (memorize — every wrong answer is one of these)
| Trap | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Unsupported** | Plausible claim the text never makes. (CB's #1 wrong answer.) |
| **Reversal / opposite** | Says the opposite of the text, or flips who-did-what. |
| **Over-reach** | Adds praise/extremes the text never asserts ("widely acclaimed," "the best," "always"). |
| **Too narrow** | True of one detail, but not the whole text. |
| **Too broad** | Bigger/more general than the text supports. |
| **Out of scope / off-task** | True or tempting, but answers a *different* question than the stem asked. |

# INFORMATION & IDEAS (≈26% / 12–14 Q)

## 1. Central Ideas (main idea)

**What it asks:** the whole-text thesis — the single point the entire blurb adds up to.

**Stems:** "Which choice best states the **main idea** of the text?" / "The main purpose... is to..." (when it's about *content*, not rhetorical function).

**Method:**
1. Read it all, then say the point in your own words: "This is about X doing/showing Y."
2. The answer must cover the **whole** passage — beginning to end — not one sentence.
3. Kill anything too narrow (one detail), too broad, or unsupported.

**Trap to watch:** **Too narrow** — a true detail dressed up as the main idea. If a choice only covers the last sentence, it's a detail, not the main idea.

**Worked example:**
> Marine biologist Ana Reyes studied the lined seahorse. Many assumed its drab brown-and-red coloring was simple camouflage. But Reyes found the colors shift with the seahorse's mood and social signaling — meaning the coloring is not just hiding, but communicating.

*Q: Main idea?*
- (A) The lined seahorse uses brown and red coloring. → **too narrow** (a detail).
- (B) Reyes's work shows the seahorse's coloring serves communication, not only camouflage. → **correct** (covers the whole arc: old assumption → finding).
- (C) Reyes is the leading expert on seahorses. → **over-reach** (text never ranks her).
- (D) Seahorse coloring is purely camouflage. → **reversal** (text overturns this).

---

## 2. Detail (command of literal content)

**What it asks:** one specific fact the text **explicitly states**.

**Stems:** "According to the text, ..." / "Based on the text, what is true about ...?"

**Method:**
1. Find the exact sentence that answers the stem. Put your finger on it.
2. The right answer is a **paraphrase** of that sentence — same meaning, different words.
3. No warrant = wrong. Don't reward "sounds right."

**Trap to watch:** **Out of scope** — a true-sounding fact that the passage never actually states. Also watch **reversal** (a detail flipped).

**Worked example:**
> The Antikythera mechanism, recovered from a Roman-era shipwreck, is an ancient Greek geared device that modeled the positions of the sun and moon. Its complexity was not matched again for over a thousand years.

*Q: According to the text, the Antikythera mechanism...*
- (A) was built by the Romans. → **reversal/out of scope** (Greek; found in a Roman-era wreck).
- (B) modeled the positions of the sun and moon. → **correct** (stated).
- (C) is the oldest known machine. → **unsupported** (never claimed).
- (D) took a thousand years to build. → **out of scope** (the thousand years is about *matching its complexity*, not building it).

## 3. Command of Evidence — Textual

**What it asks:** which quotation/finding/example would, *if true*, most directly **support / weaken / illustrate / complete** a specific claim or hypothesis.

**Stems:** "Which finding, **if true, would most strongly support** the hypothesis?" / "Which quotation **most effectively illustrates the claim**?" / "...would most directly **weaken**..."

**Method:**
1. **Nail the exact claim first** — circle the precise relationship. (e.g., "the sail helped catch *fast, evasive* prey," not just "the sail helped.")
2. Decide what you'd need: **Support** = a fact that makes the claim more likely. **Weaken** = a fact that makes it less likely / gives an alternate explanation. **Illustrate** = a concrete example of the exact concept.
3. Match the answer to the *specific* wording. Right answers hit the precise variable; traps hit a *related but different* one.

**Trap to watch:** **Off-task / near-miss** — evidence that's about the right *topic* but the wrong *part of the claim* (supports a general point, not the specific one). For "weaken" questions, a trap restates *support*.

**Worked example:**
> Researchers hypothesized that the new sail design helped the fishing boats catch *fast-swimming, evasive* species specifically, not fish in general.

*Q: Which finding, if true, would most strongly support the hypothesis?*
- (A) Total fish caught rose 20% after the new sail. → **off-task** (fish in general — not the specific claim about evasive species).
- (B) Catches of slow bottom-dwellers rose, while tuna catches were flat. → **weakens / reversal**.
- (C) Catches of fast tuna and mackerel rose sharply, while slow species were unchanged. → **correct** (hits the exact variable: fast, evasive species).
- (D) Boats traveled farther with the new sail. → **out of scope** (distance ≠ catching evasive prey).

---

## 4. Command of Evidence — Quantitative (graph/table)

**What it asks:** read a table/bar graph/line graph and pick the choice that the **data accurately support** (often to complete a sentence about the data).

**Stems:** "Which choice most effectively uses **data from the graph/table** to complete the statement / support the claim?"

**Method:**
1. **Read the graph BEFORE the choices:** title, axis labels, **units**, and what each bar/line is.
2. Re-read the sentence/claim the data must support. Note direction words: *greater than, increased, fewer, between.*
3. Check each choice against the actual numbers. The answer must be **both true per the graph AND relevant to the claim** — both halves must pass.

**Traps to watch:** (a) a number that's **accurate but irrelevant** to the claim; (b) a number that's **misread** (wrong axis, wrong series, or direction reversed — "more" when the data say "fewer").

**Worked example:**
> A table shows average daily steps: City A = 6,200; City B = 9,400; City C = 7,100. *Claim: residents of the city with the most steps walked well above the others.*

*Q: Which choice uses data from the table to support the claim?*
- (A) City A residents averaged 6,200 steps. → **accurate but irrelevant** (A isn't the most).
- (B) City B residents averaged 9,400 steps, more than 2,000 above the next-highest city. → **correct** (true AND supports "most + well above": 9,400 vs 7,100).
- (C) City C residents averaged the most steps. → **misread** (C isn't the most).
- (D) City B residents walked fewer steps than City C. → **reversal**.

---

## 5. Inference

**What it asks:** the logical conclusion the text **implies but doesn't state** — usually to finish a sentence ("Which choice most logically completes the text?").

**Stems:** "Which choice most logically **completes the text**?" / "...most reasonably **infer**...?"

**Method:**
1. Read for the **logical direction**: is the blank a continuation, a contrast (*however, but, yet*), or a cause/result (*therefore, because*)?
2. The answer must be **forced by the text** — provable from what's there, nothing added.
3. Pick the **smallest safe step**, not the boldest claim. SAT inferences are tight, not dramatic.

**Trap to watch:** **Over-reach** — a conclusion that goes further than the text guarantees (extreme words, predictions, value judgments the text didn't earn). Also watch **wrong-direction** (continues when the logic signals contrast).

**Worked example:**
> Early telescopes had small lenses that gathered little light, so faint objects stayed invisible. Newer designs use far larger mirrors. Therefore, compared with early telescopes, newer ones can ____

*Q: Most logically completes the text?*
- (A) detect fainter objects that early telescopes could not. → **correct** (bigger mirror → more light → faint objects now visible; the smallest forced step).
- (B) detect every object in the universe. → **over-reach** ("every" is unearned).
- (C) gather less light than before. → **reversal** (larger mirrors gather more).
- (D) are cheaper to build. → **out of scope** (cost never mentioned).

# CRAFT & STRUCTURE (≈28% / 13–15 Q)

## 6. Words in Context (the single biggest skill — master it)

**What it asks:** the meaning of a word/phrase **as used in this specific sentence** (often a "logical and precise" fill-in-the-blank).

**Stems:** "...most nearly means..." / "Which choice completes the text with the **most logical and precise word or phrase**?"

**Method:**
1. **Cover the choices.** Read the sentence and predict your own word for the blank.
2. Use the surrounding clues — definitions, contrasts (*although, but*), examples, and tone.
3. Match your prediction to a choice. If two seem close, the answer is the **more precise** one for *this* sentence (not just "a synonym in general").
4. Test by plugging it back in.

**Trap to watch:** **Right-meaning-wrong-fit** — a real synonym of the word in *some* context, but wrong for *this* sentence (often the dictionary's "primary" meaning when the sentence needs a secondary one). Watch the **secondary-meaning trap** for common words.

**Worked example:**
> The committee's report was deliberately ____: it raised many questions but settled none, leaving every major decision to a later meeting.

*Q: Most logical and precise word?*
- (A) thorough → **reversal** (settling none isn't thorough).
- (B) inconclusive → **correct** (matches "raised questions but settled none").
- (C) hostile → **out of scope** (no conflict shown).
- (D) brief → **unsupported** (length isn't the point; outcome is).

## 7. Text Structure & Purpose

**What it asks:** *why* the author wrote it or *what a part does* — the rhetorical function, not the content.

**Stems:** "Which choice best describes the **overall structure** of the text?" / "What is the **main purpose** of the text?" / "What is the **function** of the underlined sentence... in the text as a whole?"

**Method:**
1. Distinguish **purpose** (why the whole text exists) from **function** (the job of one part *within* the whole).
2. For function: ask "what does this sentence DO for the argument?" — set up a contrast? give an example? raise an objection? Don't just resummarize what it says.
3. Watch the verb in the answer: *to argue, to illustrate, to question, to define, to contrast.* The verb must match what the author actually does.

**Trap to watch:** **Describes content, not function** — a choice that accurately paraphrases what the sentence *says* but not what it *does*. Also **wrong verb** (says "argues" when the author merely "describes").

**Worked example:**
> Some claim social media shortens attention spans. [It is true that average video length has dropped.] However, controlled studies find no measurable decline in sustained focus.

*Q: Function of the bracketed sentence in the text as a whole?*
- (A) It states the author's final conclusion. → **wrong** (the "However" sentence is the conclusion).
- (B) It concedes a point that seems to support the claim before the author challenges it. → **correct** (it's a concession set up to be rebutted).
- (C) It provides statistical proof that attention spans are shrinking. → **over-reach / wrong verb** (it's conceded, then undercut).
- (D) It defines social media. → **out of scope**.

## 8. Cross-Text Connections (rare — usually 1–2 per test)

**What it asks:** the **relationship between two short paired texts** — how Text 2's author would respond to, agree/disagree with, or qualify Text 1.

**Stems:** "Based on the texts, **how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond** to [the view in Text 1]?" / "**relationship between** the two texts?"

**Method:**
1. Pin down **each author's specific position** in one phrase: "T1: X causes Y." "T2: actually Z."
2. Identify the relationship: agree / disagree / partly agree / one extends or qualifies the other.
3. The answer must use **Text 2's actual stated view** applied to **Text 1's actual point** — both anchors must be real. Don't invent a reaction.

**Trap to watch:** **Misattributed view** — a choice that swaps the authors' positions, or gives Text 2 an opinion it never stated. Also **overstated disagreement** (says "would reject entirely" when T2 only qualifies).

**Worked example:**
> **Text 1:** Carter argues city trees mainly benefit residents by cooling streets in summer.
> **Text 2:** Diaz notes that trees' biggest measurable benefit is actually stormwater absorption, which prevents flooding year-round; cooling is real but secondary.

*Q: How would Diaz (T2) most likely respond to Carter's (T1) claim?*
- (A) By agreeing cooling is the single most important benefit. → **reversal / misattributed**.
- (B) By acknowledging cooling matters but arguing stormwater absorption is the greater benefit. → **correct** (matches T2's "real but secondary").
- (C) By denying that trees benefit residents at all. → **overstated disagreement**.
- (D) By claiming trees harm cities. → **out of scope**.

# PRACTICE SET (12 questions — mixed, like the real test)

*Do all 12, then check. For every miss, re-read the trap rule above it. Answers + reasoning at the very bottom — don't peek.*

**Q1.** Octopuses can solve mazes and open jars, tasks that require remembering a sequence of steps. Yet each of an octopus's eight arms also contains clusters of neurons that can react to touch without input from the central brain. Researchers now suspect that octopus intelligence is distributed across the body, not concentrated in the brain alone.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?
- (A) Octopuses can open jars.
- (B) Octopus intelligence may be spread throughout the body rather than centralized in the brain.
- (C) Octopuses are the most intelligent invertebrates.
- (D) Octopus arms cannot function without the central brain.

**Q2.** In 1845, the British Franklin expedition sailed to map the Arctic's Northwest Passage and vanished. For over 160 years its two ships were lost. In 2014 and 2016, search teams using sonar located both wrecks on the Arctic seafloor.

According to the text, the Franklin expedition's ships were
- (A) found by the expedition members themselves.
- (B) located by search teams in 2014 and 2016.
- (C) never recovered.
- (D) destroyed in 1845.

**Q3.** Botanist Lena Okafor proposed that a certain desert shrub survives drought by shedding its leaves early to conserve water, rather than by storing water in its roots.

Which finding, if true, would most strongly support Okafor's hypothesis?
- (A) The shrub's roots hold more water than those of nearby plants.
- (B) Shrubs that dropped their leaves earliest survived the longest droughts, while root water content varied randomly.
- (C) The shrub grows faster in wet years.
- (D) The shrub's leaves are unusually large.

**Q4.** A line graph shows a region's wildfires per decade: 1980s = 12; 1990s = 18; 2000s = 26; 2010s = 41. *Claim: the regional wildfire rate has risen sharply over the four decades, with the steepest jump in the most recent decade.*

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to support the claim?
- (A) The region had 12 wildfires in the 1980s.
- (B) The number rose from 26 in the 2000s to 41 in the 2010s, a larger increase than in any previous decade.
- (C) Wildfires decreased in the 2010s.
- (D) The 1990s saw the largest jump.

**Q5.** Most batteries lose capacity as they are charged and discharged repeatedly. A new electrode coating, however, keeps the battery's internal structure from cracking during charging. As a result, batteries with this coating ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?
- (A) retain their capacity over more charge cycles than uncoated ones.
- (B) cannot be charged at all.
- (C) are identical to standard batteries in every way.
- (D) will never lose any capacity under any conditions.

**Q6.** The novelist's later prose grew increasingly ____: where her early sentences sprawled across half a page, her final works conveyed entire scenes in a handful of clipped words.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word?
- (A) ornate
- (B) spare
- (C) confusing
- (D) lengthy

**Q7.** Some historians argue that the printing press alone democratized knowledge in Europe. [Literacy rates did rise in the century after its invention.] But other factors — rising urban schooling and cheaper paper — were just as decisive, and the press's effect is hard to isolate.

What is the function of the bracketed sentence in the text as a whole?
- (A) It states the author's main conclusion.
- (B) It acknowledges a fact consistent with a claim the author will go on to complicate.
- (C) It proves that the printing press was the sole cause of rising literacy.
- (D) It defines what a printing press is.

**Q8.**
**Text 1:** Mendez argues that remote work raises productivity because employees avoid commutes and office distractions.
**Text 2:** Park's data show productivity gains from remote work appear mainly in the first months; over a year, gains fade as collaboration slows, though they rarely turn negative.

Based on the texts, how would Park (Text 2) most likely respond to Mendez's (Text 1) claim?
- (A) By agreeing that remote work permanently raises productivity.
- (B) By noting the productivity gains are real early on but tend to diminish over time.
- (C) By arguing remote work always reduces productivity.
- (D) By denying that commutes affect productivity.

**Q9.** A naturalist's field notes describe a bird that builds an elaborate, decorated nest not to house eggs but solely to attract a mate; once mating occurs, the structure is abandoned.

Which choice best describes the main purpose of the text?
- (A) To explain how the bird incubates its eggs.
- (B) To describe a nest built for courtship rather than for raising young.
- (C) To argue that the bird is the most skilled nest-builder.
- (D) To list the materials used in the nest.

**Q10.** The senator's speech was praised as ____: though it lasted only four minutes, it captured the entire debate's stakes in a few memorable lines.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word?
- (A) tedious
- (B) trivial
- (C) succinct
- (D) rambling

**Q11.** Coral reefs occupy less than 1% of the ocean floor, yet they shelter roughly a quarter of all marine species. Scientists warn that as warming seas cause corals to expel the algae that feed them — an event called bleaching — the loss of reef habitat could ripple far beyond the reefs themselves.

Which choice most reasonably completes the inference that bleaching is concerning because
- (A) coral reefs cover most of the ocean floor.
- (B) the species that depend on reefs could be widely affected even though reefs are small.
- (C) algae are harmful to coral.
- (D) warming seas have no effect on marine life.

**Q12.** A table shows recycling rates: Town X = 31%; Town Y = 54%; Town Z = 47%. *Claim: the town with the highest recycling rate exceeded the lowest by more than 20 percentage points.*

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the claim?
- (A) Town X recycled 31% of its waste.
- (B) Town Y, at 54%, exceeded Town X's 31% by 23 percentage points.
- (C) Town Z had the highest recycling rate.
- (D) Town Y exceeded Town Z by more than 20 points.

## ANSWER KEY + REASONING

**Q1 — B.** *(Central Ideas)* Covers the whole arc: brain-based tasks → arm neurons → distributed-intelligence conclusion. (A) too narrow (one detail). (C) over-reach ("most intelligent" — never claimed). (D) reversal (arms can react *without* the central brain).

**Q2 — B.** *(Detail)* Directly stated: "search teams using sonar located both wrecks" in 2014/2016. (A) reversal (the original members vanished). (C) reversal (they *were* found). (D) out of scope (the *expedition* vanished in 1845; the ships weren't said to be destroyed then).

**Q3 — B.** *(Textual Evidence)* The hypothesis is leaf-shedding (not root storage) drives survival. (B) hits both halves exactly: leaf-droppers survived longest AND root water didn't matter. (A) supports the *rejected* explanation. (C)/(D) out of scope (growth rate, leaf size aren't the claim).

**Q4 — B.** *(Quantitative)* The claim has two parts: sharp overall rise + steepest jump in the most recent decade. (B) cites the 26→41 jump (15) being the largest — satisfies both. (A) accurate but irrelevant (no rise shown). (C) reversal (it rose). (D) misread (the biggest jump is the 2010s, not the 1990s).

**Q5 — A.** *(Inference)* Coating prevents cracking → less degradation → keeps capacity longer. Smallest forced step. (B) reversal. (C) over-reach ("identical in every way"). (D) over-reach ("never... under any conditions" — unearned absolute).

**Q6 — B.** *(Words in Context)* Clue: early sentences "sprawled," final works used "a handful of clipped words" → predict "lean/minimal" → **spare**. (A) ornate and (D) lengthy are reversals; (C) confusing is unsupported (clipped ≠ confusing).

**Q7 — B.** *(Text Structure & Purpose / Function)* The bracketed fact (literacy rose) is *consistent with* the press-did-it claim — but the author then complicates it ("But other factors..."). It's a concession. (A) wrong (the "But" sentence carries the author's view). (C) over-reach / wrong verb ("proves... sole cause"). (D) out of scope.

**Q8 — B.** *(Cross-Text)* Park's stated view: gains are real early, fade over time, rarely negative. (B) maps exactly. (A) reversal ("permanently"). (C) overstated disagreement ("always reduces" — Park says rarely negative). (D) misattributed (Park never addresses commutes).

**Q9 — B.** *(Text Structure & Purpose / Purpose)* The text's job is to describe a courtship nest (built to attract a mate, not house eggs). (A) reversal (not for eggs). (C) over-reach ("most skilled"). (D) too narrow (materials aren't the focus).

**Q10 — C.** *(Words in Context)* Clue: only four minutes yet captured everything in a few lines → predict "brief but effective" → **succinct**. (A) tedious, (B) trivial, (D) rambling are all reversals of the praise + brevity.

**Q11 — B.** *(Inference)* Reefs are tiny (<1%) but host ~25% of marine species, so losing them ripples outward → the dependent species are widely affected despite small reef size. (A) reversal (reefs are <1%). (C) out of scope / distorts (algae *feed* coral). (D) reversal.

**Q12 — B.** *(Quantitative)* Claim = highest minus lowest > 20 points. Highest = Y (54), lowest = X (31); 54−31 = 23 > 20. (B) is true AND on-target. (A) accurate but irrelevant. (C) misread (Y is highest). (D) true number-wise (54−47=7) but doesn't address highest-vs-*lowest*, so it fails the claim.

## Quick self-scoring
- **11–12 right:** on track for a 750+ RW. Drill only your missed skill type.
- **8–10:** solid base; the misses are almost always one trap family — find which one keeps catching you (usually over-reach or off-task evidence) and target it.
- **≤7:** slow down to the 5-step loop and *predict before reading choices.* Most early misses vanish when you stop letting the choices lead you.

**Your two highest-leverage habits:** (1) predict the answer before reading the options, every time; (2) on every miss, name the trap family out loud. Do that and these ~27 questions become your strongest domain.
