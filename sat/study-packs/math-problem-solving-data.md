# Math — Problem-Solving & Data Analysis (Digital SAT)

**Why this matters for your score:** ~**15% of Math** — and it's the most *word-problem and graph-reading* heavy domain, so it's where careless misreads cost points. The math is easy; the trap is answering the wrong question or misreading a chart. Nail the reading and these are free points.

---

## The must-know concepts

### 1. Ratios, rates & proportions
Set up a proportion and cross-multiply. Watch **units** (the SAT loves unit conversions inside a rate problem).
**Worked example:** A car goes 150 miles on 5 gallons. How far on 8 gallons? → 150/5 = 30 mpg → 30·8 = **240 mi**.
**#1 trap:** flipping the ratio, or mismatched units (minutes vs hours).

### 2. Percentages & percent change
- "x% of y" = (x/100)·y. **Percent change** = (new − old)/old × 100.
- **Increase by 20%** → ×1.20; **decrease by 20%** → ×0.80. Successive changes **multiply** (a 20% rise then 20% fall is ×1.2×0.8 = 0.96, a net 4% *loss* — not 0%).
**Worked example:** A $80 item is marked up 25%, then that price is discounted 10%. Final price? → 80·1.25·0.90 = **$90**.
**#1 trap:** percent change uses the **original** as the denominator; and "percent of" vs "percent more than."

### 3. Unit conversion / dimensional analysis
Multiply by conversion fractions so units cancel. **Worked example:** 90 km/h to m/s → 90·(1000 m/km)·(1 h/3600 s) = **25 m/s**.

### 4. Statistics: center & spread
- **Mean** = sum/count; **median** = middle value (order first!); **mode** = most frequent; **range** = max − min.
- **Standard deviation** (conceptual only — no calculation): bigger spread → bigger SD. More tightly clustered data → smaller SD.
- **Mean vs median:** outliers/skew pull the **mean**, not the median. A few huge values → mean > median.
**Worked example:** Which is larger for {2, 3, 3, 4, 88}? Mean = 100/5 = 20; median = 3 → **mean is much larger** (the 88 outlier drags it up).
**#1 trap:** forgetting to **order the data** before taking the median; assuming mean = median.

### 5. Reading data displays (scatterplots, bar/line graphs, two-way tables)
- **Scatterplot + line of best fit:** use the LINE to predict; slope = rate of change; watch what the axes actually measure.
- **Two-way tables:** find the right cell; "probability that a randomly chosen X is Y" = (that cell or row) / (the correct total — row, column, or grand total depending on the wording).
**Worked example:** In a table, 30 of 50 sophomores chose option A. P(a random sophomore chose A) = 30/50 = **0.6**.
**#1 trap:** using the **wrong denominator** (grand total vs row total vs column total). Underline which group the question conditions on.

### 6. Probability
P = (favorable)/(total). Read carefully whether it's conditional ("given that…").

### 7. Sampling & inference (conceptual)
- A valid conclusion requires a **random sample**; only then can you generalize to the population.
- Larger sample / smaller margin of error = more reliable.
- An experiment can show **cause** only with random *assignment*; an observational study shows association, not cause.
**#1 trap:** over-generalizing from a non-random or small sample.

### 8. Percent/ratio in context word problems
Translate slowly: "is" → =, "of" → ×, "what" → the variable. Define the variable in the problem's units.

## Graduated practice (try, then check) — [GI] = grid-in
**P1.** A recipe serves 4 and uses 6 eggs. How many eggs to serve 10? `[GI]`
**P2.** A shirt costs $40 after a 20% discount. What was the original price?
**P3.** Data set: 4, 9, 9, 12, 16. Find the mean and the median.
**P4.** A town's population rose from 8,000 to 9,200. What was the percent increase?
**P5.** A scatterplot's line of best fit is `y = 3x + 5` (y = cost in $, x = items). Predicted cost of 10 items?
**P6.** Two-way table: of 200 surveyed, 120 are adults; 90 of the adults prefer tea. P(prefers tea | adult)?
**P7.** Convert 72 inches per second to feet per minute. `[GI]`
**P8.** Which has the larger standard deviation: {50,50,50,50} or {10,40,60,90}? Why?

### Answers
- **P1:** 6/4 = x/10 → x = 15 → **15 eggs**.
- **P2:** 40 = 0.80·orig → orig = **$50**. *(Trap: it's not $48 — don't add 20% back; divide.)*
- **P3:** mean = 50/5 = **10**; median (ordered, middle) = **9**.
- **P4:** (9200−8000)/8000 = 1200/8000 = **15%**.
- **P5:** y = 3·10 + 5 = **$35**.
- **P6:** 90/120 = **0.75** (denominator = adults, not 200).
- **P7:** 72 in/s ·(1 ft/12 in)·(60 s/min) = 6·60 = **360 ft/min**.
- **P8:** {10,40,60,90} — it's far more spread out; the first set has zero spread (SD = 0).

## Don't get these wrong
1. **Denominator discipline** on tables/probability — condition on the right group.
2. **Reverse percent** (P2): a price *after* a discount → divide, don't add the % back.
3. **Order before median.** Outliers move the mean, not the median.
4. **Successive percents multiply** (and don't cancel).
5. **Answer what's asked** — these problems bury the actual question; underline it.
