# Reading & Writing — Standard English Conventions (Grammar)

**Why this matters for your score:** This is the single highest-ROI area on the whole RW section. It's ~26% of RW (11-15 questions), every question is a fast, rule-based fill-in-the-blank, and unlike reading comprehension the answers are objectively right or wrong — once you know the rule, you get it in 15 seconds, every time. For a 1500+ target, you should be missing **zero** of these. Master the ~10 rules below and you bank time for the hard reading questions.

---

## How every SEC question looks (memorize this)

Every grammar question is fill-in-the-blank with one prompt, almost word-for-word:

> **"Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?"**

- Short passage (25-150 words), one blank, four choices.
- **The four choices differ ONLY by the rule being tested.** There is **no "NO CHANGE"** option.
- **STRATEGY — read the choices FIRST.** Scan what's changing. Punctuation shuffling around? → it's a punctuation/boundaries question. The same word in different forms (*is/are/were*, *its/their*, *who/whom*)? → it's agreement or case. Word order flipping? → modifier or parallelism. Identifying *what changes* tells you which rule to apply.
- College Board sorts these into two broad buckets: **Boundaries** (where clauses start/end and how punctuation joins or separates them) and **Form, Structure, and Sense** (the internal grammar of words/phrases — agreement, verb/pronoun form, modifiers, parallelism). Don't over-think the labels; just learn the rules.

# PART 1 — Every rule the SAT tests

Each rule: **the rule → how the SAT disguises it → worked example → the #1 trap.**

---

## RULE 1 — Independent clauses: the 5 legal joins (THE most-tested cluster)

**Rule (plain):** An independent clause is a complete sentence (subject + verb, can stand alone). You **cannot** join two independent clauses with just a comma (= *comma splice*) or with nothing (= *run-on/fused*). There are exactly **five legal ways** to connect two independent clauses:

1. **Period** → make two sentences.
2. **Semicolon** (`;`) — the two clauses must both be complete.
3. **Comma + FANBOYS** (*for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*).
4. **Colon** (`:`) — only if the first clause is complete; the second explains/specifies it.
5. **Subordinate one clause** — add *because, although, while, since, which, that…* turning it into a dependent clause.

**How the SAT disguises it:** The four choices give you the same two clauses with different punctuation in the blank: a comma, a semicolon, a comma + *and*, a period, nothing. Your job: decide whether each side is a complete sentence.

**Worked example:**
> The museum extended its hours for the exhibit ______ visitors could view the rare manuscripts after work.
> A) hours, so visitors B) hours, visitors C) hours visitors D) hours; so visitors

Left side "The museum extended its hours for the exhibit" = complete. Right side "visitors could view…" = complete. Two complete clauses → need a legal join. **A** (comma + FANBOYS *so*) works. B is a comma splice. C is a run-on. D is wrong because a semicolon can't take a conjunction after it. **Answer: A.**

**#1 trap:** Treating a **semicolon as interchangeable with a comma.** A semicolon needs a **complete sentence on BOTH sides** — it works exactly like a period. Test it: if you can replace the semicolon with a period and both halves stand alone, it's legal.

---

## RULE 2 — Semicolons (and the list exception)

**Rule (plain):** A semicolon joins two **independent clauses** (= functions as a period). Special case: it separates **list items that already contain commas** (e.g., cities with states).

**How the SAT disguises it:** Offers `;` vs `,` vs `:` in the blank. Often pairs a semicolon with a fragment to bait you.

**Worked example:**
> The recipe calls for three ingredients ______ flour, which must be sifted; sugar; and two eggs.
> A) : B) ; C) , D) —

After "ingredients" comes a list whose items contain internal commas ("flour, which must be sifted"). To introduce the list you need a colon (the first clause is complete); the **items themselves** are then separated by semicolons. The blank introduces the list → **A (colon)**. (Note the items already use semicolons because one item has a comma.)

**#1 trap:** Putting a semicolon before a **fragment.** "She loved hiking; especially in autumn" is WRONG — "especially in autumn" can't stand alone. Use a comma or dash there.

---

## RULE 3 — Colons

**Rule (plain):** A colon introduces a list, explanation, definition, or example. **The part BEFORE the colon must be a complete sentence.** Whatever comes after can be a word, phrase, or clause.

**How the SAT disguises it:** Tests whether the lead-in is complete. A colon placed mid-sentence (after a verb or preposition) is almost always wrong.

**Worked example:**
> She had one goal for the season ______ to qualify for the state championship.
> A) , B) : C) ; D) season:

"She had one goal for the season" = complete sentence → colon legally introduces the explanation. **Answer: B.**

**#1 trap:** A colon after an **incomplete** lead-in. "The ingredients are: flour, sugar, eggs" is WRONG because "The ingredients are" isn't complete. "She wanted to: run, jump, swim" is WRONG. Cover everything after the colon — if what's left isn't a full sentence, the colon is wrong.

---

## RULE 4 — Dashes

**Rule (plain):** A dash (—) can (a) replace a colon to introduce an explanation, (b) replace commas around a nonessential interruption, or (c) signal an abrupt break. **If a dash opens a mid-sentence interruption, a dash must close it** — you can't mix a dash with a comma as the pair.

**How the SAT disguises it:** Mixes punctuation pairs around an inserted phrase: dash…comma, comma…dash, dash…dash. Only the **matching pair** is correct.

**Worked example:**
> The author's first novel ______ written when she was just nineteen — became an instant bestseller.
> A) which was B) — C) , D) (

The phrase "written when she was just nineteen" is closed by a dash. So it must be **opened** by a dash to match. **Answer: B.**

**#1 trap:** **Mismatched pairs.** A nonessential phrase set off mid-sentence must use the SAME punctuation on both sides: comma…comma, dash…dash, or paren…paren. Never comma…dash.

---

## RULE 5 — Nonessential vs. essential (restrictive) elements

**Rule (plain):** Information that can be **removed without changing the core meaning** = nonessential → surround with **commas (or dashes or parentheses, in a matching pair).** Information needed to identify what you're talking about = essential → **NO commas.**

**How the SAT disguises it:** Choices add or remove commas around a phrase/name/clause. Test: delete the phrase. If the sentence still makes sense and points to the same thing, it's nonessential (commas). If removing it makes the reference vague, it's essential (no commas).

**Worked example:**
> The composer ______ wrote the symphony in 1804.
> A) Beethoven, who was nearly deaf, B) Beethoven who was nearly deaf C) Beethoven, who was nearly deaf D) Beethoven; who was nearly deaf

"who was nearly deaf" is extra info — remove it and "The composer Beethoven wrote the symphony in 1804" still works. So it's nonessential and needs commas on **both** sides. **Answer: A.**

**#1 trap:** **One comma instead of two** (choice C). A nonessential phrase needs a comma before AND after. Also beware "that" vs "which": **"that"** introduces essential clauses (no comma); **"which"** introduces nonessential (comma).

---

## RULE 6 — Subject–verb agreement

**Rule (plain):** A verb matches its subject in number: singular subject → singular verb, plural → plural.

**How the SAT disguises it:** It **separates the subject from the verb** with prepositional phrases or clauses, then offers *is/are*, *was/were*, *has/have*. The noun right before the verb is often a decoy.

**Worked example:**
> The collection of rare coins and ancient artifacts ______ displayed in the east wing.
> A) are B) were C) is D) have been

Strip the modifiers: "The collection … ______." The subject is **collection** (singular) — *of rare coins and ancient artifacts* is just a prepositional phrase. Singular subject → **C (is).**

**#1 trap:** Letting the noun nearest the verb decide. "coins and artifacts" tempts you to say *are*, but they're inside a prepositional phrase, not the subject. **Cross out everything between subject and verb**, then match. Also: collective nouns (*team, group, collection, jury*) are **singular**; indefinite pronouns (*each, everyone, neither, anyone*) are **singular**.

---

## RULE 7 — Pronoun–antecedent agreement & clarity

**Rule (plain):** A pronoun matches its antecedent in number and person. A singular noun takes *it/its*; a plural noun takes *they/them/their*. The antecedent must be clear.

**How the SAT disguises it:** Offers *it/they*, *its/their*. The antecedent is often a collective or earlier singular noun.

**Worked example:**
> Each of the participants must submit ______ registration form by Friday.
> A) their B) there C) his or her D) they're

"Each" is **singular** → needs a singular possessive. **C (his or her).** ("their" is plural; "there"/"they're" are wrong words entirely.)

**#1 trap:** Matching the pronoun to a nearby plural ("participants") instead of the true singular antecedent ("Each"). Indefinite pronouns like *each, every, anyone, someone, neither* are singular.

---

## RULE 8 — Pronoun case: subject vs. object, and who vs. whom

**Rule (plain):** Subject pronouns (*I, he, she, we, they, who*) do the action; object pronouns (*me, him, her, us, them, whom*) receive it. **Who** = subject of its clause; **whom** = object.

**How the SAT disguises it:** Offers *who/whom*, or *I/me*, *he/him* in a compound ("my brother and ___").

**Worked example:**
> The scientist ______ the committee selected will lead the expedition.
> A) who B) whom C) which D) whose

In the clause "the committee selected ___," the committee is the subject doing the selecting; the blank is the **object** of "selected" → **whom**. **Answer: B.** (Trick: rephrase — "the committee selected *him*" → *him* = object → *whom*. If it were *he*, use *who*.)

**#1 trap:** Defaulting to "who." Use the **he/him test**: rearrange the clause and substitute. *He* → *who*; *him* → *whom*. In compounds ("between you and ___"), remove the other person: "between … me," not "between … I."

---

## RULE 9 — Verb tense & form consistency

**Rule (plain):** Keep tenses consistent unless the timeline logically shifts. Use context clues (dates, time words like *currently, by 1990, now*) to pick the tense.

**How the SAT disguises it:** Offers the same verb in present, past, past-perfect, present-perfect (*writes / wrote / had written / has written*). Surrounding verbs and time markers signal the right one.

**Worked example:**
> By the time the rescue team arrived, the hikers ______ a shelter from branches.
> A) build B) have built C) had built D) are building

"By the time … arrived" (past) signals an action completed **before** another past action → past perfect. **C (had built).**

**#1 trap:** Ignoring the timeline cues. Look at the OTHER verbs and any time phrases in the sentence; the blank must agree with that established timeframe.

---

## RULE 10 — Modifiers (dangling & misplaced) — an official sample type

**Rule (plain):** A modifying word/phrase must sit right next to the noun it describes. An **introductory phrase** (especially one starting with an *-ing* or *-ed* word) must be **immediately followed by the noun it describes.**

**How the SAT disguises it:** Begins the sentence with a descriptive phrase + comma, then the four choices each start with a different noun. Pick the one that the phrase actually describes. (This is exactly the official sample Q15 type — "Based on events… , [Rabinal Achí]…".)

**Worked example:**
> Walking through the ancient ruins, ______ were clearly visible to the tourists.
> A) crumbling columns B) the tourists saw crumbling columns C) the guide pointed out crumbling columns that D) it was clear that crumbling columns

The opener "Walking through the ancient ruins" must describe whoever is walking — a **person**. Columns can't walk. Only a choice starting with a person fixes it. Of the options, the one placing a person right after the comma is correct (B puts "the tourists" as the walkers). **Answer: B.**

**#1 trap:** Picking a grammatically smooth choice whose **subject can't logically do the opening action.** Always ask: "Who or what is doing the intro phrase?" The noun right after the comma must be able to do it.

---

## RULE 11 — Parallel structure

**Rule (plain):** Items in a list or comparison must share the same grammatical form (all *-ing*, all nouns, all infinitives, etc.).

**How the SAT disguises it:** A list of two or three items; the choices vary the form of the last item. Match it to the others.

**Worked example:**
> The internship taught her to research efficiently, to write clearly, and ______ confidently.
> A) speaking B) she spoke C) to speak D) for speaking

The pattern is "to research… to write… ___ ." Parallelism demands a third **to + verb** → **C (to speak).**

**#1 trap:** Choosing the *-ing* form when the list uses infinitives (or vice versa). Find the pattern in the first items and force the blank to match.

---

## RULE 12 — Apostrophes: possessives vs. plurals & homophones

**Rule (plain):**
- Plural = add **-s**, NO apostrophe (*dogs*).
- Singular possessive = **'s** (*the dog's leash*).
- Plural possessive = s + **apostrophe** (*the dogs' leashes*).
- **its** = possessive (no apostrophe); **it's** = "it is." **their/they're/there**, **whose/who's**, **your/you're** — same logic.

**How the SAT disguises it:** Choices show the same word as plural, singular-possessive, plural-possessive, and contraction (*students / student's / students' / students're*-style traps, or *its / it's*).

**Worked example:**
> The company announced that ______ profits had doubled, surprising its investors.
> A) it's B) its C) its' D) their

Need a possessive meaning "the profits belonging to the company" (singular). **its** (no apostrophe) is the possessive. **Answer: B.** (*it's* = "it is"; *its'* is never a word.)

**#1 trap:** **its vs it's.** Substitute "it is" — if the sentence still makes sense, use *it's*; otherwise use *its*. "its' " does not exist, ever.

---

## RULE 13 — Subject–verb separation: NO punctuation (official sample Q14 type)

**Rule (plain):** Never separate a subject from its verb (or a verb from its object) with a single comma, colon, or semicolon. Sometimes the right answer is **no punctuation at all.**

**How the SAT disguises it:** This is the **official sample Q14**. The choices offer a comma, colon, semicolon, and **nothing** after a word — and "nothing" is correct because the word is the subject and the verb follows.

**Worked example:**
> The reason the experiment ultimately ______ that the temperature was never properly controlled.
> A) failed, was B) failed: was C) failed; was D) failed was

The subject "The reason… failed" connects directly to its verb "was." You can't separate subject from verb with punctuation → **D (no punctuation).**

**#1 trap:** Feeling like you "need" punctuation because the sentence is long. Don't. If the blank sits between a subject and its verb, the answer is usually **no punctuation.**

---

## NOT grammar — don't confuse these (Transitions)

Words like *however, therefore, in fact, for example, meanwhile* are **Transitions** — a separate RW skill (Expression of Ideas) testing **logic between sentences**, not grammar. If the four choices are different linking words (and punctuation stays the same), it's a transition question: figure out the **relationship** (contrast? cause? addition? example?) between the two sentences, then pick the connector that matches. Don't apply grammar rules there.

# PART 2 — Graduated practice set (answers after)

*Do all 12 first. Difficulty rises. Then check the worked answers.*

**Q1.** The library renovated its reading room ______ the study carrels remain closed until spring.
A) room, B) room; C) room D) room: but

**Q2.** Maria packed three things for the trip ______ a map, a compass, and a water bottle.
A) : B) ; C) , D) ;

**Q3.** The novel's protagonist ______ a quiet bookkeeper — undergoes a dramatic transformation.
A) , B) — C) ; D) (

**Q4.** A flock of geese ______ across the autumn sky each morning.
A) fly B) flies C) are flying D) have flown

**Q5.** Neither of the proposals ______ enough funding to move forward.
A) have B) were having C) has D) are

**Q6.** The director, along with the lead actors, ______ attending the premiere tonight.
A) are B) is C) were D) have been

**Q7.** Every student in the advanced classes must bring ______ own laptop to the exam.
A) their B) there C) his or her D) they're

**Q8.** The mentor ______ the students admired most retired last year.
A) who B) whom C) which D) whose

**Q9.** Before she became a senator, Chen ______ as a civil-rights attorney for two decades.
A) works B) has worked C) had worked D) is working

**Q10.** Determined to finish the marathon, ______ despite the cramping in her legs.
A) the finish line was Ana's focus B) Ana pushed forward C) it was important to Ana to continue D) Ana's legs kept moving

**Q11.** The workshop covered how to draft a résumé, how to network effectively, and ______ for interviews.
A) preparing B) how to prepare C) you should prepare D) preparation

**Q12.** The committee released ______ findings after months of deliberation, and the public welcomed the report.
A) it's B) its C) their D) its'

---

## Worked answers

**Q1 — B.** "The library renovated its reading room" and "the study carrels remain closed" are both complete clauses. Two independent clauses need a legal join; a **semicolon** works. A is a comma splice; C is a run-on; D wrongly puts a conjunction after a colon. *(Rule 1/2)*

**Q2 — A (colon).** "Maria packed three things for the trip" is a complete sentence, and a list of explanation follows → **colon**. The list items are separated by plain commas (none contain internal commas), so semicolons (B/D) are wrong. *(Rule 3)*

**Q3 — B (dash).** The nonessential phrase "a quiet bookkeeper" is **closed** by a dash ("— undergoes"), so it must be **opened** by a dash to match. A comma…dash mismatch (A) is illegal. *(Rule 4)*

**Q4 — B (flies).** Subject is **flock** (singular collective noun); "of geese" is a prepositional phrase. Singular → *flies*. The plural "geese" is a decoy. *(Rule 6)*

**Q5 — C (has).** "Neither" is a **singular** indefinite pronoun; "of the proposals" is just a prepositional phrase. Singular → *has*. *(Rule 6/7)*

**Q6 — B (is).** The subject is **director** (singular). "along with the lead actors" is a nonessential interrupter set off by commas — it does NOT change the subject's number. Singular → *is*. (Phrases like *along with, as well as, in addition to* don't make a singular subject plural.) *(Rule 6)*

**Q7 — C (his or her).** "Every student" is **singular**, so the possessive must be singular. "their" is plural; "there"/"they're" are wrong words. *(Rule 7)*

**Q8 — B (whom).** In the clause "the students admired ___ most," the students are the subject doing the admiring; the blank is the **object** of "admired." He/him test: "the students admired *him*" → *him* → **whom**. *(Rule 8)*

**Q9 — C (had worked).** "Before she became a senator" sets up an action completed **before** a past event → **past perfect** "had worked." The time cue "Before… became" drives it. *(Rule 9)*

**Q10 — B (Ana pushed forward).** The opener "Determined to finish the marathon" must describe a **person** who is determined. A finish line, "it," and legs can't be "determined." Only "Ana" can — and she must come right after the comma. A, C, and D are dangling modifiers. *(Rule 10 — official sample type)*

**Q11 — B (how to prepare).** The list pattern is "how to draft… how to network… ___ ." Parallelism requires a third "**how to** + verb." The other forms break the pattern. *(Rule 11)*

**Q12 — B (its).** Need a **possessive** meaning "the findings belonging to the committee" (a singular collective noun → *its*). "it's" = "it is" (fails the substitution test); "their" is plural; "its' " isn't a word. *(Rule 12)*

# PART 3 — Quick rules checklist (last-minute scan)

- [ ] **Read the answer choices first** — what's changing tells you the rule.
- [ ] **Two complete sentences?** → only 5 legal joins: period · semicolon · comma+FANBOYS · colon · subordinate one clause. A lone comma = comma splice (wrong).
- [ ] **Semicolon = period.** Needs a complete sentence on BOTH sides. (Exception: separating list items that contain commas.)
- [ ] **Colon** needs a **complete sentence before it.** Cover what follows — if the lead-in isn't a full sentence, the colon is wrong.
- [ ] **Dashes/commas/parens around an interruption come in MATCHING PAIRS.** Never comma…dash.
- [ ] **Nonessential info → two commas** (or two dashes). Test by deleting it. "that" = essential (no comma); "which" = nonessential (comma).
- [ ] **Subject-verb agreement:** cross out everything between subject and verb, then match. Collective nouns & *each/every/neither/anyone* = **singular**. *Along with / as well as* don't change the subject's number.
- [ ] **Pronoun agreement:** match the TRUE antecedent's number, not the nearest noun.
- [ ] **who vs. whom:** he/him test. *He* → who (subject); *him* → whom (object).
- [ ] **Verb tense:** match the sentence's other verbs and time cues. "By the time…[past]" → past perfect "had ___."
- [ ] **Modifiers:** the noun right after an intro phrase must be able to DO that phrase. Ask "who/what is doing this?"
- [ ] **Parallelism:** match the form of the other list items (all *to ___*, all *-ing*, all nouns).
- [ ] **its** = possessive; **it's** = "it is" (substitution test). **its'** is never a word. Plurals don't take apostrophes.
- [ ] **Don't separate a subject from its verb** with punctuation — sometimes the answer is **NO punctuation.**
- [ ] **Different linking words (however/therefore/for example)?** That's a **Transition** (logic), not grammar — pick by the relationship between sentences.

**Bottom line:** These ~13 rules cover essentially every SEC question. Drill them until each is automatic, and aim to spend ≤20 seconds per grammar question so you can pour your time into the harder reading items. This is where a 1500+ scorer wins easy points.
