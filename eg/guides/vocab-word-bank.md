## Week 1

SAT Vocabulary: Week 1
 
1. sycophant (n): one who is eager to please for personal gain; or more informally, a suck-up or brown-noser
   * Jillian is such a sycophant; she is always telling the teacher how great he is just so she can get a better grade, and then she goes home and insults him behind his back!


2. lugubrious (adj.): extremely sad, gloomy, or mournful
   * After the car hit my puppy, I felt lugubrious for days until he was fully healed; I never thought I could feel so depressed!


3. pernicious (adj): causing great harm & destruction, wicked
   * I hate the vile girl; her pernicious rumors destroyed my reputation!


4. insouciant (adj): unconcerned or unworried, often to the point of arrogance
   * That insouciant boy walks around like nothing ever bothers him; this cavalier confidence may be attractive to girls, but it drives teachers crazy.


5. ubiquitous (adj): present everywhere at once, omnipresent
   * Starbucks are so ubiquitous; I see one around every corner and every street!


6. non sequitur (n): a statement that is totally unrelated to the statement that it follows
   * “Are you even listening?” screamed Helen. “That non sequitur had nothing to do with what I was just saying!”


7. restive (adj): uneasy, agitated; having little patience; unwilling to be controlled
   * The restive mob was on the edge of anarchy; the riot police had to use tear-gas to get them to disperse.


8. diatribe (n): A bitter verbal or written attack on someone or something
   * After they all cheated on the final exam, the teacher unleashed a diatribe on the class that included insults that went too far.


9. jingoistic (adj): extremely fervent and patriotic, to the point of even expressing hostility toward other countries.
   * The jingoistic soccer fans waved American flags and burned a flag of the opposing team; their patriotism went a little too far.


10. implacable (adj): not easily calmed or pacified; impossible to change or move
   * I was so stubborn that my anger was completely implacable; nothing could change my mind or calm me down.

## Week 2

SAT Vocabulary List #2
 
1. hirsute (adj): covered with hair or having a large amount of hair
   * Kendall didn’t even need to shave until he was in his mid-twenties, and then he got so hirsute, he had to shave twice a day!
2. affinity (n): a natural liking for or inclination toward somebody or something, particularly because you identify with that person/thing
   * After only know Jeff for 10 minutes, I immediately had an affinity for him; I liked him right off because it was clear we had so much in common.
3. accede (v): to give consent or agreement to something
   * Considering the leader has so much power, I think it best to accede to his wishes; I am worried about what will happen if we don’t agree to his demands.
4. bonhomie (n): easy & good-humored friendliness
   * It is very important to me that my classes all have a feeling of bonhomie in them; a friendly and welcome environment is the first step in creating a good place to learn.
5. quotidian (adj): ordinary & commonplace, particularly because it occurs on a daily basis
   * I am tired of listening to the announcements every morning; they are nearly the same each day and have become so very quotidian.
6. coquette (n): a flirt
   * She may be a coquette, but she sure knows how to get what she wants when it comes to men; what a flirt!
7. harangue (v): to criticize or question somebody in an angry way
   * After I arrived home an hour after my curfew without calling, my parents harangued me for another hour; they lectured and yelled until I nearly snapped.
8. bovine (adj): displaying the slowness regarded as being typical of cattle
   * Because I only slept three hours in the last two days, I am feeling rather bovine and sluggish; I can barely put one foot in front of the other.
9. facetious (adj): intended to be humorous, not to be taken seriously
   * “I was just being facetious!” remarked Sally. “Don’t take me so seriously when you know I am just kidding!”
10. inchoate (adj): just beginning to develop, only partly formed (said: “in-co-hit”)
   * I am worried that my essay is due tomorrow because it is only in inchoate form; my rough draft looks for like an outline. I hope I organize and finish it by tomorrow.

## Week 3

SAT Vocabulary: Week #3
 
1. contrite (adj): genuinely & deeply sorry about something
   * After screaming at my sister and calling her names, I felt truly CONTRITE; I was so sorry for hurting her feelings.
2. erudite (adj): having great knowledge gained from study & reading
   * Reverend Hale thought he was so ERUDITE because of his many books on demons and witchcraft; unfortunately, all this knowledge and learning still didn’t allow him to see the truth before his eyes.
3. anomaly (n): something that deviates from the norm or expectations
   * Jalen getting an F on his math test was a complete ANOMALY; he typically scores so well in Calculus, so we were shocked at this break from the norm.
4. cantankerous (adj): easily angered & difficult to get along with
   * My CANTANKEROUS  neighbor is such a grump! No matter how often I smile at her and try to make friendly conversation, she just yells out venomous complaints.
5. coterie (n): a small, exclusive group of people who share the same interests
   * On the first Tuesday of every month, I go out to dinner with my COTERIE; we love chatting about all our common interests.
6. deride (v): to ridicule/insult someone or something
   * I am so tired of the way my brother DERIDES everything I do; he constantly insults and ridicules me!
7. feckless (adj): lacking the thought or organization necessary to succeed, incompetent due to an inability to do anything useful
   * That FECKLESS woman was no help at all because she is just so unorganized and has no idea what she is really supposed to be doing.
8. excoriate (v): to criticize and/or scold somebody very strongly  
   * After my dog urinated on the carpet, I EXCORIATED her for her bad behavior; her tail was under her legs after that scolding.
9. debacle (n): something that becomes a defeat or humiliating failure
   * The dance was nothing but a massive DEBACLE; the whole front row of dancers fell off the stage and the back row didn’t even know the moves. What a massive failure!
10. temerity (n): reckless confidence that might be offensive
   * That jerk had the TEMERITY to assume Jillian would marry him after he cheated on her three times; he was so confident she would say yes he already booked the hall for the reception!

## Week 5

SAT Vocabulary List #5
 
1. augment (verb): to grow/increase something in number, amount, size, strength, or intensity.
   * To AUGMENT my income, I am going to start tutoring; I need to grow my salary and make some more money to pay my bills.
2. insidious (adj): slowly and subtly harmful or destructive
   * Shelly may not think her rumor was all that harmful, but it was truly INSIDIOUS; that small comment slowly destroyed Chloe’s reputation.
3. kismet (noun): fate or destiny
   * Bumping into Joey was pure KISMET; we were destined to meet because we’ve never been apart since, and he is my soulmate.
4. enmity (noun): extreme hatred, particularly between enemies
   * Even though they competed fiercely, there was no ENMITY between the opponents; the match was over, and they were back to being friends, no hatred remaining
5. denizen (noun): somebody who is in a place or area and/or a regular visitor to a place
   * The DENIZENS of the restaurant always enter with a smile and a warm hello; it is clear they come here often.
6. fallacious (adj): deceptive AND liable to mislead people because of a mistaken belief or idea
   * The belief that spanking children leads to better behavior is entirely FALLACIOUS, causing many parents to hit their children out of a belief that it is beneficial; in reality, spanked children perform lower in school and exhibit nearly twice the rates of discipline problems.
7. obfuscate (verb): to make something confusing and unclear, especially by making it unnecessarily complicated.
   * I hate filling out legal paperwork; they clearly complicated all the wording in order to OBFUSCATE people, leading many to agree to terms they should not out of a lack of understanding.
8. malaise (noun): a general feeling of worry/depression, resulting is lethargy OR a general feeling of illness or sickness without any specific diagnosis.
   * The constant rain left the town’s denizens in a state of MALAISE; everywhere I went, people moped around and moved in a bovine fashion.
9. peculate (verb): to get money or property by embezzlement or theft
   * The teller planned to PECULATE money from people’s accounts; he got caught before he could embezzle any real money, though.
10. efficacious (adj): having the power to produce the desired result
   * The politician was so skilled in rhetoric that all his speeches were entirely EFFICACIOUS; he got the crowd to believe exactly what he wanted them to and eventually won the tightly contested election.
