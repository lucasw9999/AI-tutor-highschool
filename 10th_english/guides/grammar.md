# Grammar & Mechanics — for the cumulative grammar exams + clean essays

**Why this matters for your grade:** In Mrs. Pagtakhan's class, the cumulative grammar exams test these exact rules directly, and every analytical essay (Caesar eulogy, *Night*, *Book Thief*, *Kite Runner*, *Life of Pi*) loses easy points to comma splices, wordiness, and apostrophe slips. Master the twelve things below and you protect your grade on two fronts at once: you ace the grammar tests *and* your essays read clean, which nudges borderline grades up.

**How to use this:** Don't read it like a textbook. Cover the answers, hit the practice items, then check yourself. Re-drill anything you miss the night before an exam. Each section = the rule, the traps, wrong-vs-right examples, then drills with answers.

---

## 0. The fastest essay fix: the RIP-words editing pass

Before any essay goes in, do one editing pass where you **R**emove, **I**mprove, or **P**ower-up weak words. This single discipline kills most of the errors below.

- **R — Remove filler:** *really, very, so, just, actually, basically, in order to, the fact that, due to the fact that, there is/there are.*
- **I — Improve vague words:** *thing, stuff, a lot, good, bad, big* → swap for something specific.
- **P — Power-up dead verbs:** *is/are/was/were + noun* → one strong verb. ("makes a decision" → "decides"; "is reflective of" → "reflects.")

**Drill it:** Read your draft out loud. Every time you stumble or run out of breath, there's a comma splice, run-on, or wordy clause hiding there.

## 1. Active vs. Passive Voice (and why passive = wordiness)

**The rule:** In **active** voice the subject *does* the action: *Brutus betrayed Caesar.* In **passive** voice the subject *receives* the action, using a form of *to be* + past participle: *Caesar was betrayed by Brutus.* Active is shorter, clearer, and stronger — prefer it.

**Spot passive fast:** look for a *be*-verb (*is, are, was, were, been, being*) + a past participle (*betrayed, written, taken*), often followed by "by ___."

**Common errors**
- Defaulting to passive in analysis ("It is shown by the author that…") — wordy and weak.
- Thinking *every* "was" is passive. *Caesar was ambitious* is **not** passive — it's a linking verb + adjective, and it's fine.
- Overcorrecting: passive is sometimes the right choice (when the doer is unknown or unimportant: *The body was discovered at dawn.*).

**Examples (wrong/weak vs. right/strong)**
- Passive/wordy: *The theme of guilt is explored by Wiesel throughout the memoir.*
  Active/tight: *Wiesel explores guilt throughout the memoir.*
- Passive: *A decision to flee was made by the family.*
  Active: *The family decided to flee.*
- Passive: *Mistakes were made.* (vague — who?)
  Active: *The council made mistakes.*

**Practice (rewrite in active voice):**
1. The eulogy was delivered by Antony to manipulate the crowd.
2. The novel is narrated by Death.
3. It was decided by the senators that Caesar must die.
4. Many symbols are used by the author to develop the theme.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Antony delivered the eulogy to manipulate the crowd.
2. Death narrates the novel.
3. The senators decided that Caesar must die.
4. The author uses many symbols to develop the theme.
</details>

## 2. Affect vs. Effect

**The rule (99% of cases):**
- **Affect** = a **verb** meaning *to influence*. ("The war **affects** Eliezer's faith.") → *A* for *Action*.
- **Effect** = a **noun** meaning *a result*. ("The war has a lasting **effect** on his faith.") → *E* for the *End result*.

**The rare exceptions (know them for the exam, but they're uncommon):**
- **Effect** *can* be a verb meaning *to bring about*: "The senators hoped to **effect** change." (= cause it to happen)
- **Affect** *can* be a noun in psychology meaning *emotional expression*: "The patient showed a flat **affect**." (You'll almost never need this one.)

**Common errors**
- "This **effects** the reader." ✗ → influence = verb = **affects**.
- "The **affect** of the speech was powerful." ✗ → result = noun = **effect**.

**Quick test:** Can you put *the* or *an* in front? → noun → **effect**. Is it the action happening to something? → verb → **affect**.

**Examples**
- Wrong: *Antony's words deeply effect the mob.* → Right: *Antony's words deeply **affect** the mob.*
- Wrong: *The affect of the betrayal is tragic.* → Right: *The **effect** of the betrayal is tragic.*
- Right (both, side by side): *How the famine **affects** Pi is the central **effect** Martel wants us to feel.*

**Practice (choose affect or effect):**
1. The author's tone has a chilling ______ on the reader.
2. Fear ______ every choice the family makes.
3. The medicine had no ______.
4. The new law will ______ how students cite sources. *(harder)*
5. The senators wanted to ______ real change in Rome. *(tricky)*

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **effect** (noun — "an effect")
2. **affects** (verb — to influence)
3. **effect** (noun — "no effect")
4. **affect** (verb — to influence how)
5. **effect** (rare verb use — to bring about)
</details>

## 3. Commas & Comma Splices

**The rule — five comma jobs you actually need:**
1. **Items in a series:** *He was brave, loyal, and naive.* (Use the Oxford comma before *and* — it's the MLA/academic standard and it prevents ambiguity.)
2. **Before a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS) joining two independent clauses:** *Caesar refused the crown, but the crowd cheered.* (FANBOYS = **F**or, **A**nd, **N**or, **B**ut, **O**r, **Y**et, **S**o.)
3. **After an introductory word/phrase/clause:** *After the assassination, the city erupted.*
4. **Around non-essential information (interrupters):** *Brutus, who loved Rome, still struck the blow.* (Drop the part between commas and the sentence still makes sense.)
5. **Don't** put a comma between subject and verb: *Brutus, killed Caesar.* ✗

**Comma splice = the #1 essay killer.** A comma splice joins two complete sentences with only a comma. That's not strong enough.
- Splice: *Caesar was ambitious, the senators feared him.* ✗

**Four ways to fix a comma splice:**
1. **Period:** *Caesar was ambitious. The senators feared him.*
2. **Semicolon** (when ideas are closely related): *Caesar was ambitious; the senators feared him.*
3. **Comma + FANBOYS:** *Caesar was ambitious, so the senators feared him.*
4. **Subordinate one clause:** *Because Caesar was ambitious, the senators feared him.*

**Common errors**
- Using a comma before *however, therefore, thus, then* to join sentences (those are NOT FANBOYS): *He lied, however no one noticed.* ✗ → *He lied; however, no one noticed.* ✓
- Comma before *because* in a normal sentence: *He fled, because he was afraid.* usually needs no comma → *He fled because he was afraid.*

**Examples**
- Wrong: *Liesel steals books, they give her power.* → Right: *Liesel steals books; they give her power.* (or use a period)
- Wrong: *The plan failed therefore they regrouped.* → Right: *The plan failed; therefore, they regrouped.*
- Right (series): *Amir feels guilt, shame, and a desperate need to atone.*

**Practice (fix the comma error; more than one fix can be correct):**
1. Pi survives the wreck, he is alone with a tiger.
2. The speech was short however it changed everything.
3. He was loyal brave and honest.
4. After Hassan left Amir felt relief and dread.
5. Antony, manipulated the crowd with irony.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. *Pi survives the wreck, **but** he is alone with a tiger.* (or semicolon, or period)
2. *The speech was short**; however,** it changed everything.* ("however" is not a FANBOYS — needs a semicolon)
3. *He was loyal**,** brave**,** and honest.* (series needs commas)
4. *After Hassan left**,** Amir felt relief and dread.* (comma after the introductory clause)
5. *Antony manipulated the crowd with irony.* (no comma between subject and verb)
</details>

## 4. Apostrophes (possession vs. contraction vs. plain plural)

**The rule:**
- **Possession, singular:** add **'s** — *Caesar's death, Wiesel's faith, the witness's account.*
- **Possession, plural ending in -s:** add just **'** — *the senators' votes, the soldiers' boots.*
- **Possession, plural NOT ending in -s:** add **'s** — *the children's fear, the people's anger.*
- **Contraction:** the apostrophe marks missing letters — *it's = it is/has, don't = do not, they're = they are.*
- **Plain plural:** **NO apostrophe** — *books, ideas, the 1990s, the Romans.*

**The three killer pairs (memorize cold):**
| Word | Means | Test |
|------|-------|------|
| **its** | belonging to it | possessive — like *his/hers* (no apostrophe) |
| **it's** | it is / it has | can you expand to "it is"? |
| **your** | belonging to you | possessive |
| **you're** | you are | expand to "you are"? |
| **whose** | belonging to whom | possessive |
| **who's** | who is / who has | expand to "who is"? |

**Common errors**
- *Its raining* ✗ → *It's raining* (it is). | *The dog wagged it's tail* ✗ → *its tail.*
- Apostrophe in a plain plural: *Banana's for sale* ✗ → *Bananas.*
- *Theirs / yours / hers / ours* never take an apostrophe.

**Examples**
- Wrong: *The novels themes are dark.* → Right: *The novel's themes are dark.*
- Wrong: *Its a story about survival.* → Right: *It's a story about survival.*
- Right (plural possessive): *The prisoners' silence said everything.*

**Practice (fix the apostrophe; mark "correct" if fine):**
1. The book thief steals to feed her familys hope.
2. Its clear that Death is the narrator.
3. The two brothers loyalty is tested.
4. Whose going to deliver the eulogy?
5. The 1940s were brutal years.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. *…her **family's** hope.* (singular possessive)
2. ***It's** clear…* (it is)
3. *The two **brothers'** loyalty…* (plural possessive)
4. ***Who's** going…* (who is)
5. **Correct** — plain plural decade, no apostrophe.
</details>

## 5. Lie vs. Lay

**The rule:** This is mostly a **conjugation** problem.
- **Lie** = to recline / rest (no object — you can't "lie" *something*). Parts: **lie / lay / (have) lain / lying.**
- **Lay** = to put or place *something* down (needs a direct object — you lay *something*). Parts: **lay / laid / (have) laid / laying.**

**The trap that ruins everyone:** the present tense of *lay* is spelled the same as the **past tense of *lie***. "I **lay** down yesterday" is correct (past of *lie*). "I **lay** the book down now" is also correct (present of *lay*, with object "book").

| Meaning | Present | Past | Past participle | -ing |
|---------|---------|------|-----------------|------|
| recline (no object) | lie | **lay** | (have) lain | lying |
| place something (object) | lay | laid | (have) laid | laying |

**Common errors**
- *I'm going to lay down.* ✗ → *I'm going to **lie** down.* (no object)
- *Yesterday he laid on the cot.* ✗ → *Yesterday he **lay** on the cot.* (past of *lie*, no object)
- *She had laid awake all night.* ✗ → *She had **lain** awake all night.*
- *Lay the wreath on the grave.* ✓ (correct — "wreath" is the object)

**Examples**
- Wrong: *Pi lays in the lifeboat, exhausted.* → Right: *Pi **lies** in the lifeboat, exhausted.* (present, no object)
- Wrong: *The soldier had laid in the field for hours.* → Right: *The soldier had **lain** in the field for hours.*
- Right: *The mourners **laid** flowers at Caesar's feet.* (past, object = flowers)

**Practice (choose the correct form):**
1. Each night Eliezer would (lie / lay) awake, afraid.
2. Please (lie / lay) the documents on the desk.
3. Yesterday the dog (lay / laid) in the sun all afternoon.
4. The body had (laid / lain) undiscovered for days.
5. She (lay / laid) the baby in the crib an hour ago.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **lie** (recline, no object, present)
2. **lay** (place something — "documents" is the object)
3. **lay** (past of *lie* — no object; "laid" would need an object)
4. **lain** (past participle of *lie*, no object)
5. **laid** (past of *lay* — "baby" is the object)
</details>

## 6. Parallel Structure

**The rule:** Items in a list, a comparison, or a paired construction must share the same grammatical form — all nouns, all *-ing* verbs, all infinitives, all clauses. Mixing forms breaks the parallel and sounds off.

**Common errors**
- Mixing *-ing* with infinitives: *She likes reading, writing, and **to debate**.* ✗ → *reading, writing, and **debating**.* ✓
- Breaking parallel after correlative pairs (*not only…but also, either…or, both…and*): *Caesar was not only **a general** but also **ruled** Rome.* ✗ → *not only **a general** but also **a ruler**.* ✓
- Lists where one item is a clause and the rest are phrases.

**Examples**
- Wrong: *The novel explores guilt, loss, and how people survive.*
  Right: *The novel explores **guilt, loss, and survival**.* (all nouns)
- Wrong: *Antony came to bury Caesar, to praise Rome, and **flattering** the mob.*
  Right: *Antony came **to bury** Caesar, **to praise** Rome, and **to flatter** the mob.* (all infinitives)
- Wrong: *Liesel learns by reading books and **she steals** them.*
  Right: *Liesel learns by **reading** books and **stealing** them.* (parallel *-ing*)

**Practice (fix the parallelism):**
1. Pi's survival depends on faith, courage, and being resourceful.
2. The eulogy was moving, persuasive, and it manipulated people.
3. Amir wants not only to forget the past but also escaping his guilt.
4. To win, to lead, and conquering were Caesar's ambitions.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. *…faith, courage, and **resourcefulness**.* (all nouns)
2. *The eulogy was moving, persuasive, and **manipulative**.* (all adjectives)
3. *…not only to forget the past but also **to escape** his guilt.* (matching infinitives after the correlative pair)
4. ***To win, to lead, and to conquer** were Caesar's ambitions.* (all infinitives)
</details>

## 7. Semicolons & Colons

**Semicolon ( ; ) — two jobs:**
1. **Join two closely related independent clauses** (each could stand alone as a sentence): *Caesar was warned; he ignored the soothsayer.*
2. **Separate list items that already contain commas** (a "super-comma"): *The trip covered Rome, Italy; Paris, France; and Cairo, Egypt.*
   - Also used before conjunctive adverbs joining clauses: *He lied; **however,** no one noticed.*

**Colon ( : ) — one main job:** introduce a list, explanation, or quotation **after a complete sentence.** *Antony had one goal: revenge.* / *The memoir asks a brutal question: where was God?*

**The make-or-break test:**
- **Semicolon:** must have a **complete sentence on BOTH sides.**
- **Colon:** must have a **complete sentence BEFORE it** (what comes after can be a fragment/list).

**Common errors**
- Semicolon joining a fragment: *Caesar was ambitious; which scared the senators.* ✗ ("which scared the senators" isn't a sentence) → use a comma.
- Colon after an incomplete lead-in: *The themes are: guilt, faith, and survival.* ✗ ("The themes are" isn't a complete sentence) → *The novel has three themes: guilt, faith, and survival.* ✓ (or just drop the colon).

**Examples**
- Wrong: *Liesel loves words, they are her weapon.* → Right: *Liesel loves words; they are her weapon.* (fixes the splice — both sides complete)
- Wrong: *Pi packs everything he needs: like water and biscuits.* → Right: *Pi packs everything he needs: water and biscuits.* (no "like" after a colon)
- Right (colon for emphasis): *Death reveals the truth early: Liesel will outlive them all.*

**Practice (semicolon, colon, or neither — fix it):**
1. Brutus made his choice ___ he would kill his friend for Rome.
2. The essay needs three things ___ a claim, evidence, and analysis.
3. Caesar was warned ___ which he ignored.
4. Wiesel survives ___ but he is forever changed.
5. The reader feels one thing above all ___ dread.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **semicolon** — both sides are complete sentences.
2. **colon** — "The essay needs three things" is a complete sentence introducing a list.
3. **comma** (neither) — "which he ignored" is a fragment, so a semicolon is wrong: *Caesar was warned, which he ignored.*
4. **comma** — "but" is a FANBOYS joining two clauses: *Wiesel survives, but he is forever changed.* (A semicolon would also work, but then drop "but.")
5. **colon** — complete sentence before, single word after for emphasis.
</details>

## 8. Run-Ons & Awkward Syntax

**The rule:** A **run-on (fused sentence)** jams two independent clauses together with no punctuation: *Caesar was ambitious the senators feared him.* ✗ A **comma splice** (Section 3) is the same crime with a weak comma. Fix both the same four ways: **period, semicolon, comma+FANBOYS, or subordination.**

**Awkward syntax** = grammatically "legal" but clunky: misplaced modifiers, words in a confusing order, or a sentence that loses its own track.

**Common errors**
- **Fused sentence:** *He drew his sword the crowd gasped.* ✗ → *He drew his sword, **and** the crowd gasped.*
- **Misplaced modifier:** *Running for the boat, the rain soaked Pi.* ✗ (the rain isn't running) → *Running for the boat, **Pi** was soaked by the rain.* / *As Pi ran for the boat, the rain soaked him.*
- **Dangling modifier:** *After reading the letter, the truth was clear.* ✗ (who read it?) → *After reading the letter, **Amir** understood the truth.*

**Fix-it move:** put the **doer of the opening phrase right after the comma.** An *-ing* or *-ed* phrase at the start must describe the subject that follows it.

**Examples**
- Run-on: *Liesel reads aloud the neighbors listen.* → Right: *Liesel reads aloud**;** the neighbors listen.*
- Awkward (modifier): *Covered in blood, the senators fled the scene.* (Were the senators covered in blood, or Caesar?) → *The senators fled the scene, leaving Caesar covered in blood.*
- Awkward: *The reason Antony spoke was because he wanted revenge.* (redundant) → *Antony spoke because he wanted revenge.*

**Practice (fix the run-on or awkward sentence):**
1. Pi prayed every day he refused to give up.
2. Walking onto the stage, the speech began.
3. The senators stabbed Caesar he fell at Pompey's statue.
4. Hidden in the basement, the war could not reach Liesel for a while.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. *Pi prayed every day**;** he refused to give up.* (or period / "and") — fused sentence.
2. ***Walking onto the stage, Antony began the speech.*** (the speech can't walk — put the doer after the comma)
3. *The senators stabbed Caesar**, and** he fell at Pompey's statue.* (fused sentence — add comma+FANBOYS, semicolon, or period)
4. ***Hidden in the basement, Liesel was safe from the war for a while.*** (Liesel is hidden, not the war — fix the dangling modifier)
</details>

## 9. Wordiness & Redundancy

**The rule:** Cut every word that doesn't add meaning. Tight writing reads as confident and smart; padded writing reads as filler. This is where the **RIP pass** (Section 0) earns its keep, and it directly raises essay scores.

**Common wordiness traps → fix:**
| Wordy | Tight |
|-------|-------|
| due to the fact that / because of the fact that | because |
| in order to | to |
| at this point in time | now |
| the reason is because | because |
| in the event that | if |
| has the ability to | can |
| make a decision | decide |
| is reflective of / is indicative of | reflects / shows |
| there are many critics who argue | many critics argue |
| in today's society / in this day and age | today (or cut it) |

**Redundancy (saying it twice) → fix:**
- *past history* → history | *each and every* → each (or every) | *final outcome* → outcome | *completely destroyed* → destroyed | *very unique* → unique (unique is absolute) | *advance planning* → planning | *true fact* → fact | *end result* → result.

**Common errors**
- Stacking qualifiers: *very really quite important* → *important* (or *crucial*).
- Restating the prompt as your thesis instead of making a claim.
- *"This quote shows that…"* → just analyze; you don't need the announcement.

**Examples**
- Wordy: *Due to the fact that Caesar was ambitious, in order to protect Rome the senators made the decision to kill him.*
  Tight: *Because Caesar was ambitious, the senators killed him to protect Rome.*
- Redundant: *The final outcome of the war completely destroyed his faith.*
  Tight: *The war destroyed his faith.*
- Wordy: *There are many symbols that the author uses in order to show the theme of survival.*
  Tight: *The author uses many symbols to show survival.*

**Practice (tighten each sentence):**
1. Due to the fact that Pi is alone, he has the ability to survive only by means of his faith.
2. In today's modern society, each and every reader can relate to the theme of guilt.
3. The reason Amir betrays Hassan is because of the fact that he is a coward.
4. The author makes use of foreshadowing in order to create a feeling of suspense.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. *Because Pi is alone, he survives only through his faith.*
2. *Today, every reader can relate to the theme of guilt.* (or cut "today" entirely)
3. *Amir betrays Hassan because he is a coward.*
4. *The author uses foreshadowing to create suspense.*
</details>

## 10. Subject–Verb Agreement

**The rule:** A verb must match its subject in number — singular subject → singular verb, plural subject → plural verb. The trap is almost never the obvious case; it's what sits *between* the subject and the verb.

**Common errors**
- **Prepositional-phrase distractor:** the noun right before the verb isn't the real subject. *Each of the senators* **were** *afraid.* ✗ → "each" is singular, not "senators" → **was**.
- **Compound subject with "and":** normally plural. *Brutus and Cassius* **plans** *the murder.* ✗ → **plan**.
- **"Or"/"nor" subjects:** the verb agrees with the subject **closer to it**. *Neither the soldiers nor Brutus* **know** *the outcome.* ✗ → agree with "Brutus" (closer) → **knows**.
- **Collective nouns:** singular when acting as one unit. *The crowd* **were** *chanting.* ✗ (one crowd, one action) → **was** *chanting.*
- **Indefinite pronouns that are ALWAYS singular:** *each, either, neither, everyone, everybody, someone, nobody, anyone.* → *Everyone* **have** *a theory.* ✗ → **has**.

**Examples**
- Wrong: *The list of conspirators **were** long.* → Right: *The list of conspirators **was** long.* ("list" is the subject, not "conspirators")
- Wrong: *Neither Portia nor the servants **knows** the truth.* → Right: *Neither Portia nor the servants **know** the truth.* ("servants," the closer noun, is plural)
- Right: *The senate **votes** as one body on the matter.* (collective noun, singular verb)

**Practice (choose the correct verb):**
1. Each of the conspirators (has / have) a reason to fear Caesar.
2. Brutus, along with Cassius, (plan / plans) the assassination. *(tricky — "along with" is not "and")*
3. Either the omens or Calpurnia's dream (convinces / convince) Caesar to almost stay home.
4. The audience (watches / watch) as one when Antony reveals the will.
5. Everybody in the crowd (believe / believes) Antony by the end of the speech.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **has** ("each" is singular; "of the conspirators" is a distractor phrase)
2. **plans** ("along with Cassius" is a parenthetical, not a true compound subject — the true subject is singular "Brutus")
3. **convinces** (with "either/or," the verb agrees with the closer subject — "Calpurnia's dream," singular)
4. **watches** ("audience" is a collective noun acting as one unit)
5. **believes** ("everybody" is always singular)
</details>

## 11. Pronoun–Antecedent Agreement

**The rule:** A pronoun (he, she, it, they, their, its...) must match its **antecedent** (the noun it replaces) in number *and* — for people — logically in the way it refers to them. The most common essay trap is a **singular indefinite antecedent paired with a plural pronoun.**

**Common errors**
- **Singular indefinite antecedents treated as plural:** *everyone, each, either, neither, a person, the reader, the author* are singular — pairing them with "they/their" is the #1 trap in analytical writing. *Each character reveals **their** flaw.* (Traditionally singular-required: **his or her** flaw, or better, rewrite the sentence to avoid the issue: *The characters reveal their flaws.*)
- **Ambiguous antecedent:** the pronoun could point to more than one noun. *When Brutus met Cassius, **he** already suspected treachery.* ✗ (who suspected — Brutus or Cassius?) → name the person: *When Brutus met Cassius, Cassius already suspected treachery.*
- **Collective noun mismatch:** *The senate raised **their** hands.* ✗ (one body) → **its** hands (or rewrite: *The senators raised their hands.*)
- **"Which/who/that" trap:** use *who* for people, *which/that* for things. *The soldier which died* ✗ → *The soldier **who** died.*

**The cleanest fix for the #1 trap:** don't force "he/she" or clunky "he or she" — instead make the antecedent **plural** so "they" is correct, or rewrite around the pronoun entirely.

**Examples**
- Wrong: *Every senator feared for **their** life.* → Right (plural fix): *All the senators feared for **their** lives.* → Right (singular fix): *Every senator feared for **his** life.* (only if the group is known to be all male, as in the play's senate)
- Wrong: *The reader forms **their** own opinion of Brutus.* → Right: *Readers form **their** own opinions of Brutus.*
- Ambiguous: *Antony told Octavius **he** would rule Rome.* → Clear: *Antony told Octavius that Octavius would rule Rome.*

**Practice (fix the agreement or ambiguity error):**
1. Each of the wives senses danger, but their husbands ignore it.
2. Cassius is the conspirator which convinces Brutus to join the plot.
3. When Amir betrays Hassan, he never fully recovers. *(who is "he"? fix the ambiguity)*
4. A tragic hero is undone by their own flaw.
5. The crew debated among themselves before it made a decision. *(collective noun check)*

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. *Each of the wives senses danger, but **her husband** ignores it.* (keep it singular to match "each" — or rewrite: *The wives sense danger, but their husbands ignore it.*)
2. *Cassius is the conspirator **who** convinces Brutus to join the plot.* ("who" for a person, not "which")
3. *When Amir betrays Hassan, **Amir** never fully recovers.* (name the subject — "he" was ambiguous)
4. *A tragic hero is undone by **his or her** own flaw.* (or rewrite plural: *Tragic heroes are undone by their own flaws.*)
5. *The crew debated among themselves before **the crew** made a decision.* (collective noun acting as one unit takes a singular pronoun — "it" is already correct; the fix is on the verb form if anything, not the pronoun)
</details>

## 12. Verb-Tense Consistency

**The rule:** Pick one tense for your analysis — literary analysis is conventionally written in the **literary present** (Brutus *kills* Caesar, not *killed*) — and stay in it. Don't drift between past and present within the same discussion unless the timeline genuinely requires it (e.g., describing something that happened *before* the main story's "present").

**Common errors**
- **Random tense-shifting inside one sentence/paragraph:** *Brutus **kills** Caesar and then **fled** to his tent.* ✗ (present → past for no reason) → *Brutus **kills** Caesar and then **flees** to his tent.*
- **Summarizing the plot in past tense, then shifting to present for analysis** without a clear reason. Pick present for both, or be deliberate: past tense is fine for a true narrative retelling, but literary analysis essays should default to **present tense** throughout.
- **Real exception — flashbacks/backstory:** if the text itself moves backward in time (e.g., a character remembering an earlier event), the earlier event can correctly stay in **past tense** while the frame narrative stays present. *Amir **recalls** how he **had run** from the alley.* (present frame + past-perfect memory is correct, not an error)

**Examples**
- Wrong: *Liesel steals the book, and later she **returned** it.* → Right: *Liesel steals the book, and later she **returns** it.*
- Wrong (drifting): *Pi prays daily. He **prayed** because faith **keeps** him sane.* → Right: *Pi **prays** daily. He **prays** because faith **keeps** him sane.*
- Right (legitimate past-inside-present): *Amir **confesses** that he **had watched** Hassan's attack without acting.* (the attack is backstory to the confession — past-perfect is correct here)

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

2. *Antony delivers his eulogy, and the crowd **riots**.* (stay present)

## One-page cheat sheet (cram the night before)

- **Active > passive.** Subject does the action. Kills wordiness.
- **Affect** = verb (influence). **Effect** = noun (result). "An effect" → noun.
- **Comma splice** = two sentences + comma. Fix: period / semicolon / comma+FANBOYS / subordinate.
- **FANBOYS** = For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So. *However/therefore* are NOT FANBOYS → use a semicolon.
- **its** = possessive, **it's** = it is. Same for **your/you're**, **whose/who's**.
- **Plain plurals get NO apostrophe** (books, the 1940s).
- **Lie** (recline, no object): lie/lay/lain. **Lay** (place something, has object): lay/laid/laid.
- **Parallel:** all items same form (nouns with nouns, *-ing* with *-ing*, infinitives with infinitives).
- **Semicolon:** complete sentence on BOTH sides. **Colon:** complete sentence BEFORE it.
- **Run-on:** two sentences fused. Opening modifier must describe the subject right after the comma.
- **Subject-verb agreement:** ignore distractor phrases between subject and verb; "each/either/neither/everyone" are always singular; or/nor agrees with the closer noun.
- **Pronoun-antecedent agreement:** watch singular indefinites ("everyone," "each") paired with "their" — fix by pluralizing the antecedent or naming the noun instead of the pronoun.
- **Verb tense:** literary analysis defaults to **present tense** throughout; don't drift to past mid-paragraph (backstory/flashback in past tense is a legitimate exception).
- **RIP pass:** Remove filler, Improve vague words, Power-up dead verbs. Read it aloud.
