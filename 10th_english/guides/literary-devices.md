# Literary, Rhetorical & Poetic Devices — Reference + Recognition Practice

**Why this matters for your grade:** Mrs. Pagtakhan's device decks and the Lit-Terms Final test one skill above all — can you *name the device when you see it* and *explain why the author used it*. Naming devices correctly is also the move that turns a B-range literary-analysis essay into an A one, because it lets you write "Brutus uses anaphora to..." instead of "Brutus repeats words."

**How to use this guide:** Read a group, then *cover the answers* and quiz yourself with the 20-question test at the end. Don't just memorize definitions — practice the recognition, because that's exactly what the test asks. For every device, learn three things: (1) the one-line definition, (2) what it looks like in a real text, (3) **why** an author reaches for it (this is the part that earns essay points).

> **Quick test-taking trick — the 3 questions to ask yourself:**
> 1. Is it about **persuasion / argument**? → look in the Rhetorical group.
> 2. Is it about **how the story or play is built / who's speaking**? → Narrative/Dramatic group.
> 3. Does it have to do with **sound, rhythm, or line breaks**? → Sound/Poetry group.

## GROUP 1 — Rhetorical & Persuasive Devices
*(These show up most in the* Julius Caesar *speeches, the Eulogy essay, and the MLA argument essay.)*

### Aristotle's three appeals (ethos, pathos, logos)
The three ways a speaker persuades an audience.

- **Ethos** — appeal to **credibility/character** ("trust me, I'm honorable").
  - *Example (Julius Caesar):* Antony repeatedly calls himself plain and Brutus "honourable" — and reminds the crowd "I speak not to disprove what Brutus spoke" — building his own trustworthiness before he attacks.
- **Pathos** — appeal to **emotion** (pity, fear, anger, pride).
  - *Example (Julius Caesar):* Antony shows the crowd Caesar's bloody, torn mantle and his will leaving money to every citizen, deliberately provoking grief and rage.
- **Logos** — appeal to **logic/reason** (facts, evidence, cause-and-effect).
  - *Example (Julius Caesar):* Antony argues, "When that the poor have cried, Caesar hath wept... Was this ambition?" — using evidence to logically dismantle Brutus's claim that Caesar was ambitious.
- **Why authors use them:** Persuasion almost never works on one channel. Antony's funeral oration is the textbook case of stacking all three to turn a crowd. (On your essays, *name the appeal* — graders love it.)

### Syllogism
A three-part logical argument: **major premise + minor premise → conclusion.**
- *Example:* "All men are mortal (major). Caesar is a man (minor). Therefore Caesar is mortal (conclusion)." Brutus reasons this way to justify the assassination: ambition is dangerous → Caesar may become ambitious → therefore Caesar must die.
- **Why authors use them:** To make a conclusion feel logically forced and unavoidable — and Shakespeare lets us notice when a premise is shaky (Caesar *wasn't* yet ambitious), exposing flawed reasoning.

### Anaphora
Repeating the **same word(s) at the START of successive clauses/lines.**
- *Example (Julius Caesar):* Antony's drumbeat "**Brutus** is an honourable man; / So are they all, all **honourable men**" — and his repeated "I" statements.
- **Why authors use it:** Builds rhythm and emphasis; the repetition hammers an idea in and (in Antony's case) lets repetition curdle a compliment into sarcasm.

### Antistrophe (a.k.a. epistrophe)
Repeating the **same word(s) at the END of successive clauses/lines** — the mirror image of anaphora.
- *Example:* "Where now? Who now? When now?" reversed — e.g., "...government **of the people**, **by the people**, **for the people**." Repeated end-word "people."
- **Why authors use it:** The repeated ending lands like a hammer-blow, making each phrase feel like a return to the same powerful idea.

### Antithesis
Putting **two opposite ideas together in balanced, parallel structure.**
- *Example (Julius Caesar):* "Not that I loved Caesar **less**, but that I loved Rome **more**." (Brutus)
- **Why authors use it:** The contrast sharpens both ideas and makes a line memorable and quotable — perfect for speeches.

### Apostrophe (rhetorical)
Directly addressing someone **absent or dead, or an abstract idea/object,** as if it could respond. *(Not the punctuation mark!)*
- *Example (Julius Caesar):* Antony alone with the corpse: "O, **pardon me, thou bleeding piece of earth**..." — speaking to Caesar's body.
- **Why authors use it:** Heightens emotion and lets a character externalize private feeling; signals that strong passion has overflowed.

### Paralepsis (a.k.a. paralipsis / occupatio)
Emphasizing something by **pretending to pass over it** ("I won't even mention...").
- *Example (Julius Caesar):* Antony: "Let but the commons hear this testament... / **I do not mean to read it**" — then he reads the will anyway. Saying he won't makes them beg for it.
- **Why authors use it:** Sneaky persuasion — you raise the point *and* look modest/reluctant while doing it.

### Synecdoche
A **part stands for the whole** (or whole for a part).
- *Example (Julius Caesar):* "Lend me your **ears**" — "ears" stands for the whole act of attention/listening.
- **Why authors use it:** Compresses meaning and creates a vivid image with one word.

### Metonymy
Substituting a word with **something closely associated** with it (not a literal part).
- *Example (Julius Caesar):* "The **Senate** has decided" (the building/institution stands for the senators); "the **crown**" for royal power.
- **Why authors use it:** Efficient and evocative — lets one associated word carry a whole concept.
- **Synecdoche vs. metonymy (test trap):** Synecdoche = a literal *part* of the thing ("ears" are part of a body). Metonymy = something *associated* but not a part ("crown" is not part of a king).

## GROUP 2 — Narrative & Dramatic Devices
*(Heavy in* Julius Caesar*,* Night*,* The Book Thief*,* The Kite Runner*,* Life of Pi*. Big on characterization questions and Socratic seminars.)*

### Dramatic tragedy & the tragic hero
- **Tragedy:** a serious drama in which the protagonist falls from high standing to ruin/death.
- **Tragic hero:** a basically noble character undone by a **tragic flaw (hamartia)** — often pride — leading to a downfall, a moment of recognition, and our pity/fear (catharsis).
- *Example (Julius Caesar):* Brutus — honorable and well-meaning, but his flaws (idealism, poor judgment, trusting Antony, misreading people) doom him and Rome. He dies recognizing his error.
- **Why authors use it:** The fall of a good-but-flawed person is more tragic and more instructive than the fall of a villain; it makes us examine our own flaws.

### Foil
A character who **contrasts** with another to highlight that other's qualities.
- *Example (Julius Caesar):* Cassius (cunning, emotional, pragmatic) is a foil to Brutus (idealistic, principled, naive). *Life of Pi:* the two contrasting castaways/stories foil each other.
- **Why authors use it:** Contrast clarifies a character — we understand Brutus's idealism better *because* Cassius's scheming sits next to it.

### Soliloquy
A character **alone on stage speaking thoughts aloud** — no one else hears.
- *Example (Julius Caesar):* Brutus alone in his orchard: "It must be by his death..." — we watch him talk himself into the conspiracy.
- **Why authors use it:** Gives the audience direct access to a character's private reasoning and inner conflict.

### Monologue
A **long speech by one character TO OTHERS** (who are present).
- *Example (Julius Caesar):* Antony's funeral oration ("Friends, Romans, countrymen...") — delivered to a crowd.
- **Why authors use it:** Lets a character develop a full argument or emotion at length and dominate a scene.
- **Soliloquy vs. monologue (test trap):** Soliloquy = alone, thinking aloud, no audience inside the play. Monologue = others are listening.

### Aside
A **brief remark a character makes that other characters on stage don't hear** — often to the audience.
- *Example (drama):* A character mutters a true opinion to the audience while pretending to agree with someone on stage.
- **Why authors use it:** Reveals a character's real thoughts/intentions and creates dramatic irony.

### Irony (three kinds)
- **Verbal irony** — saying the **opposite** of what you mean (sarcasm is one type).
  - *Example (Julius Caesar):* Antony calling Brutus "an **honourable** man" while proving he's a murderer.
- **Situational irony** — the **outcome is the opposite** of what's expected.
  - *Example (Julius Caesar):* Caesar ignores every warning (the soothsayer, Calpurnia's dream) because he thinks staying home looks fearful — and going out gets him killed.
- **Dramatic irony** — the **audience knows something a character doesn't.**
  - *Example (Julius Caesar):* We hear the conspirators plot; Caesar greets them warmly, unaware they're about to stab him.
- **Why authors use it:** Irony creates tension, humor, or tragedy, and rewards the attentive reader/viewer with a layer of meaning the characters miss.

### Pun
A play on words using **double meaning or similar sound** for wit.
- *Example (Julius Caesar):* The cobbler in Act 1 calls himself a "mender of **bad soles**" (soles/souls) and one who lives by the "**awl**" (awl/all).
- **Why authors use it:** Humor, cleverness, and sometimes a sly second meaning hiding in a joke.

### Malapropism
**Mistakenly using a wrong word that sounds similar** to the right one — usually for comic effect.
- *Example (general):* "He is the very **pineapple** of politeness" (meant *pinnacle*).
- **Why authors use it:** Comedy and characterization — it marks a character as bumbling or pretentious.

### Characterization (direct & indirect; STEAL)
How an author reveals what a character is like.
- **Direct:** the narrator/text **tells you outright** ("Brutus was an honorable man").
- **Indirect:** the text **shows you** and you infer — remember **STEAL**:
  - **S**peech — what they say
  - **T**houghts — their inner thoughts
  - **E**ffect on others — how others react to them
  - **A**ctions — what they do
  - **L**ooks — their appearance
- *Example (The Book Thief):* We learn Hans Hubermann is gentle and brave through his **actions** (teaching Liesel to read at night, giving bread to a Jewish prisoner) — indirect characterization — not because the narrator labels him "kind."
- **Why authors use it:** Indirect characterization makes characters feel real and lets readers *discover* them; direct characterization is fast and efficient.

### Diction
An author's **word choice** (formal/informal, harsh/gentle, simple/elevated, etc.).
- *Example (Night):* Wiesel's stripped, plain, almost numb diction ("Never shall I forget that night, the first night in camp...") mirrors the deadening of feeling the Holocaust forced on him.
- **Why authors use it:** Word choice sets tone and reveals attitude — *which* words an author picks is itself an argument about how to feel.

### Syntax
**Sentence structure** — length, order, and arrangement of words.
- *Example (Night):* Wiesel's short, fragmented sentences create a clipped, breathless, traumatized rhythm. *(The Book Thief:* Zusak uses fragments and one-line paragraphs for emphasis.)
- **Why authors use it:** Syntax controls pace and emphasis — short sentences feel urgent/shocked; long flowing ones feel reflective or overwhelming.
- **Diction vs. syntax (test trap):** Diction = *which words.* Syntax = *how the words are arranged.*

## GROUP 3 — Sound & Poetry Devices
*(For the May Poetry Project, the Lit-Terms Final, and any poem on a reading quiz.)*

### Alliteration
Repetition of the **same initial consonant sound** in nearby words.
- *Example:* "**B**etween the **b**lood-**b**lackened..." — repeated *b* sound at word-beginnings.
- **Why authors use it:** Musicality, emphasis, and linking words together so they stick in the ear.

### Assonance
Repetition of **vowel sounds** inside nearby words.
- *Example:* "The l**igh**t of the f**i**re is a sight" — repeated long-*i* vowel.
- **Why authors use it:** Creates internal music and mood without full rhyme.

### Consonance
Repetition of **consonant sounds**, especially at the **ends/middles** of words (not just the start).
- *Example:* "The lumpy, bumpy road" — repeated *mp* / *p* sounds inside the words.
- **Why authors use it:** Subtle sonic texture that knits a line together.
- **Alliteration vs. consonance (test trap):** Alliteration is consonants at the *beginning*; consonance is consonant sounds *anywhere* (often the end).

### Cacophony
A deliberately **harsh, clashing, unpleasant sound** in a line (hard *k, g, t, d, x* sounds).
- *Example:* "The **cl**ang and **cl**atter of the **cr**a**ck**ed bell."
- **Why authors use it:** To make harsh, chaotic, or violent content *sound* harsh — form matching meaning.

### Euphony
**Smooth, pleasant, harmonious sound** (soft *l, m, n, r,* long vowels).
- *Example:* "The **l**ull of the **l**ow, **m**urmuring strea**m**."
- **Why authors use it:** To make peaceful, beautiful, or soothing content *sound* lovely. (Euphony and cacophony are opposites.)

### Euphemism
A **mild or indirect word substituted for one that's harsh or blunt.**
- *Example (Night):* The Nazis' clinical language — "selection," "the chimney," sending people "to the showers" — euphemisms that hide mass murder behind neutral words.
- **Why authors use it:** To soften, to be polite, or (chillingly) to disguise something horrific — Wiesel exposes how euphemism enabled atrocity.

### Elision
**Omitting a sound or syllable** so a word fits the meter (usually marked with an apostrophe).
- *Example (Julius Caesar):* "o'er" for *over*, "'tis" for *it is*, "ne'er" for *never*.
- **Why authors use it:** To keep a line on its metrical beat without changing the meaning.

### Apocope
Dropping the **final** sound/syllable of a word.
- *Example:* "morn" for *morning*, "oft" for *often*, "th'" for *the*.
- **Why authors use it:** Fits the meter and gives an older or compressed poetic feel. *(It's a specific kind of elision — cut from the* end*.)*

### Syncope
Dropping a sound/syllable from the **middle** of a word.
- *Example (Julius Caesar):* "ta'en" for *taken*, "e'er" for *ever*, "o'er" for *over*.
- **Why authors use it:** Same as apocope — preserves meter — but the cut is in the *middle*.
- **Apocope vs. syncope (test trap):** Apocope cuts the **end**; syncope cuts the **middle**.

### Scansion & metrical feet
**Scansion** = marking the stressed (´) and unstressed (˘) syllables in a line. A **foot** is one unit of that rhythm. The common feet:
| Foot | Pattern | Sound | Example word |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Iamb** | ˘ ´ | da-DUM | "be-LONG" |
| **Trochee** | ´ ˘ | DUM-da | "GAR-den" |
| **Anapest** | ˘ ˘ ´ | da-da-DUM | "in-ter-RUPT" |
| **Dactyl** | ´ ˘ ˘ | DUM-da-da | "EL-e-phant" |
| **Spondee** | ´ ´ | DUM-DUM | "HEART-BREAK" |

The number of feet per line: **pentameter** = 5 feet. So **iambic pentameter** = 5 iambs (da-DUM ×5), the heartbeat rhythm of Shakespeare.
- *Example (Julius Caesar):* "But **soft**, what **light** through **yon**der..." pattern — *(general Shakespeare iambic pentameter):* "shall **I** com-**pare** thee **to** a **sum**mer's **day**?"
- **Why authors use it:** Meter creates a controlled musical rhythm; breaking the expected beat draws attention to a key word or moment.

### Enjambment
A line of poetry that **runs on to the next line with NO punctuation/pause** at the line break.
- *Example:*
  > I think that I shall never see
  > A poem lovely as a tree.
  (the thought flows across the break)
- **Why authors use it:** Creates momentum and surprise, and can make a key word land at the start of the next line.

### Caesura
A **strong pause IN THE MIDDLE of a line** (often marked by a period, comma, dash, or colon).
- *Example (Julius Caesar):* "Et tu, Brute? || Then fall, Caesar." — the pause after "Brute?" is the caesura.
- **Why authors use it:** Forces a dramatic break that emphasizes the words around it and varies the rhythm.
- **Enjambment vs. caesura (test trap):** Enjambment = no pause at the *line's end* (it runs on). Caesura = a pause in the *middle* of a line.

### Anastrophe
**Inverted/unusual word order** (out of normal subject-verb-object).
- *Example (Star Wars Yoda is the famous one):* "Powerful you have become." *In verse:* "Cowards die many times before their deaths" reordered for stress — or "**A peace above all earthly dignities**" placed before its subject.
- **Why authors use it:** Fits meter/rhyme, emphasizes a word by moving it, and creates an elevated, poetic voice.

### A few general poetry terms (good to know for the final)
- **Stanza** — a grouped set of lines (a poem's "paragraph").
- **Meter** — the overall rhythmic pattern of stresses.
- **Rhyme scheme** — the pattern of end-rhymes, labeled with letters (ABAB, AABB...).
- **Free verse** — poetry with no regular meter or rhyme.
- **Imagery** — language that appeals to the five senses.
- **Metaphor** — a direct comparison (X *is* Y); **Simile** — comparison using *like* or *as*.
- **Personification** — giving human qualities to non-human things. *(In* The Book Thief*, **Death itself narrates** — a sustained personification.)*
- **Symbolism** — an object/image standing for a larger idea *(e.g.,* Life of Pi*'s lifeboat; the* Kite Runner*'s kite = innocence, guilt, redemption).*
- **Tone** — the author's attitude toward the subject. **Mood** — the feeling created in the reader.

### The 6 pairs the test loves to confuse (memorize these contrasts)
1. **Synecdoche vs. metonymy** — part of the thing vs. associated with the thing.
2. **Soliloquy vs. monologue** — alone (no listeners) vs. spoken to others.
3. **Diction vs. syntax** — which words vs. how arranged.
4. **Alliteration vs. consonance** — consonants at the start vs. consonants anywhere.
5. **Enjambment vs. caesura** — run-on line-end vs. pause mid-line.
6. **Apocope vs. syncope** — cut the end vs. cut the middle. (Both are kinds of elision.)

### How to bank these into your essays (the actual grade-raiser)
Don't just name the device — finish the sentence with **effect**: *"Shakespeare uses [device] to [effect], which shows [bigger meaning/theme]."*
Example: *"Antony's anaphora of 'honourable man' uses verbal irony to turn the crowd against Brutus, revealing how rhetoric can weaponize praise."* That one sentence pattern, repeated with the right device names, is what moves an analysis essay into the A range.
