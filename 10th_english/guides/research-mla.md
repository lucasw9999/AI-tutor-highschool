# MLA Research Paper & Works Cited — the Partner Research Essay

**Why this matters for your grade.** This essay is a two-month unit (Jan–Feb) and one of the few times all semester your grade depends not just on *what* you argue but on whether your sources are real, evaluated, and cited correctly — Mrs. Pagtakhan runs everything through turnitin.com, so sloppy or missing citations don't just lose style points, they read as plagiarism and tank the whole grade. The good news: citation is the most *learnable* part of the rubric — it's a fixed set of rules, so if you drill the patterns below you can lock in those points cold while everyone else is guessing.

> **How to use this (don't read it like a lecture).** This guide is built around *doing*. Read Section 1–2 once to set up the argument, then live in Sections 5–7 (in-text citations, Works Cited, quote integration) — those are pure pattern-matching and that's where points are won or lost. Do the 3 practice problems at the end with the answers covered. If you can format a book, a website, and a database article from memory, you've beaten 90% of the class.

This guide follows **MLA 9th edition** (the current standard) and the exact class process: **pick a topic → library databases → research → outline (with commentary, 2:1 ratio) → multiple drafts → Works Cited page → turnitin.com.**

---

## 1. First: this is still an *argument*, not a fact dump

The #1 mistake on a research paper is treating it like a report — "here are five facts about my topic." That earns a C. A research *essay* makes a **debatable, analytical claim** and uses sources as **evidence and authority**, not as the point itself. It's the same analytical move you make on the *Night* and *Book Thief* essays — claim, evidence, commentary — except now the evidence comes from outside experts instead of the novel.

**Report vs. argument:**

| Report (weak) | Argument (strong) |
|---|---|
| "Social media use among teens has risen since 2010." | "Although social media is blamed for teen anxiety, the more decisive factor is *sleep disruption*, which social media accelerates but does not solely cause." |
| Lists what sources say. | Uses sources to *support a position the writer is taking*. |
| No "so what." | Every fact answers "so what does this prove?" |

**The 2:1 commentary ratio (from Mrs. Pagtakhan's outline).** For every line of evidence (a quote or paraphrased fact), you owe **two lines of your own analysis** — explaining what it means and how it proves your thesis. Sources are witnesses; *you* are the lawyer. The essay is graded on your reasoning, not on how many quotes you can stack.

## 2. Build a thesis-driven research argument

Work in this order:

1. **Pick a topic you can argue, then narrow it.** "Climate change" is a library, not a topic. "Whether carbon taxes or subsidies more effectively cut emissions" is an essay. Narrow until there are clearly *two sides*.
2. **Turn it into a question.** *Does X cause Y? Is X or Y the bigger factor? Should we do X?* A research question keeps you hunting for evidence instead of summarizing.
3. **Do enough reading to form a tentative answer.** That answer is your **working thesis**. It's allowed to change as you research — in fact it *should*.
4. **Write a thesis that names your claim AND your reasoning.** Weak: "Vaccines are important." Strong: "Mandatory school vaccination has done more to eradicate measles than improved treatment has, because prevention breaks transmission chains that treatment cannot reach."

**Thesis test:** Could a smart person disagree? If no, it's a fact, not a thesis. Make it arguable.

## 3. Finding & evaluating sources

**Start where the class tells you: the library databases**, not Google. Databases (e.g., **Gale, EBSCO, JSTOR, Opposing Viewpoints, ProQuest** — whatever Foothill's library subscribes to) give you *vetted, citable* articles and usually generate a citation for you. A random blog does not.

**The CRAAP test — evaluate every source on five things:**

- **C — Currency.** How recent is it? For science/tech/policy, prefer the last ~5 years.
- **R — Relevance.** Does it actually address *your* question, at the right depth (not a kids' encyclopedia, not a 90-page dissertation)?
- **A — Authority.** Who wrote it? What are their credentials? Is it a `.edu`, `.gov`, established news org, or peer-reviewed journal?
- **A — Accuracy.** Are claims sourced/cited? Can you verify them elsewhere?
- **P — Purpose.** Why does this exist — to inform, or to sell/persuade/push an agenda? Watch for bias.

**Source quality, ranked (high → low):**
1. Peer-reviewed journal articles (via database)
2. Books from real publishers; government/.gov data; major reputable news
3. Articles on reputable organization sites (.org with a clear, credible author)
4. *(Risky — verify hard)* general websites with named authors
5. **Avoid for citing:** Wikipedia (use it only to find *its* sources at the bottom), random blogs, anonymous pages, AI chatbots. **Wikipedia is a starting point, never a cited source.**

**Aim for variety:** most teachers want a *mix* — e.g., at least one book, one database article, and one website. Check your assignment sheet for the exact minimum (often 3–5 sources). When in doubt, ask Mrs. Pagtakhan for the required count and types.

## 4. Taking notes (so you don't accidentally plagiarize)

Plagiarism usually isn't evil — it's a **note-taking failure**. You forget which words were yours and which were the source's. Prevent it at the note stage.

**The 3-column note method.** For each useful idea, record:

| Quote / paraphrase | My own commentary | Source + page/location |
|---|---|---|
| "direct words in quotation marks" — OR — paraphrase in *your own* words | what this proves for my thesis | Author last name, p. # (or paragraph/URL) |

**Rules that save you:**
- **Always put quotation marks around copied words IMMEDIATELY**, even in private notes. This one habit prevents most plagiarism.
- **Paraphrase = rewrite the *idea* in your own sentence structure and words**, not just swap a few synonyms. If 3+ words in a row match the original, it's a quote — punctuate it as one or rewrite it fully.
- **Record the citation info the moment you take the note.** Hunting for it later is how sources go missing.
- **Track the page number / paragraph** for everything — you need it for the in-text cite.

## 5. In-text (parenthetical) citations — the daily workhorse

**Every** fact, quote, statistic, or idea that isn't your own original thought or common knowledge needs an in-text citation. The MLA system is **author + page number**, and it points the reader to the matching full entry on your Works Cited page.

**The core rule:** `(LastName page#)` — **no comma, no "p."**

> The frontal lobe is "the last region of the brain to fully mature" (Jensen 42).

**Author named in the sentence (signal phrase)?** Then put *only* the page number in parentheses:

> Jensen argues that the frontal lobe is "the last region of the brain to fully mature" (42).

**No page number (most websites)?** Just give the author — or, if no author, a shortened title. **Do NOT invent page or paragraph numbers from your browser.**

> Teen sleep loss is now treated as a public-health issue (Carter).
> Screen time before bed delays melatonin release ("Sleep and Screens").

**Two authors:** `(Smith and Lee 88)`. **Three or more:** `(Smith et al. 88)`.

**Quick reference:**

| Situation | In text |
|---|---|
| Author + page | `(Jensen 42)` |
| Author in signal phrase | `Jensen ... (42)` |
| No author (use short title) | `("Sleep and Screens")` |
| Web source, no pages | `(Carter)` |
| Two authors | `(Smith and Lee 88)` |
| 3+ authors | `(Garcia et al. 211)` |

**Golden link:** the **first word** of your parenthetical must be the **first word** of the matching Works Cited entry. That's how the reader finds the full source. If your cite says `(Jensen 42)`, the Works Cited entry must start with `Jensen`.

## 6. Integrating + citing quotes (don't "drop" quotes)

A **dropped quote** — a quotation slammed into the paragraph with no lead-in — is a classic grade-killer. Every quote needs a **frame**: introduce it, quote it, then explain it (the 2:1 commentary).

**Weak (dropped):**
> Teen brains are still developing. "The frontal lobe is the last region of the brain to fully mature" (Jensen 42).

**Strong (framed + analyzed):**
> Neurologist Frances Jensen explains that "the frontal lobe is the last region of the brain to fully mature" (42). Because this region governs impulse control and risk assessment, her finding suggests that holding teenagers to fully adult standards of judgment is biologically unrealistic — a point that reframes the entire debate over the driving age.

Notice: **signal phrase → quote → 2 sentences of *your* analysis.** That's the 2:1 ratio in action.

**Mechanics:**
- **Short quote (≤ 4 lines):** keep it in your sentence with quotation marks. Period goes *after* the parenthetical: `... mature" (Jensen 42).`
- **Long quote (> 4 lines):** **block quote** — start a new line, indent the whole quote 0.5", no quotation marks, and the period goes *before* the parenthetical. (Use these sparingly; one max in a short essay.)
- **Changing a quote:** use `[brackets]` to add/alter a word for grammar, and `...` (ellipsis) to cut words. Don't change the meaning.
- **Prefer paraphrase + analysis over long quotes.** Graders reward your thinking, not your transcription.

## 7. The Works Cited page — formatting + the 3 you'll use most

**Page formatting (memorize these — they're free points):**
- Starts on its **own new page** at the end.
- Title **`Works Cited`** centered at top — **no bold, no italics, no underline, no quotes.**
- **Double-spaced** throughout, no extra blank lines between entries.
- **Hanging indent:** first line flush left, every following line indented 0.5".
- **Alphabetical** by the first word of each entry (usually author's last name; if no author, by title — ignore *A/An/The*).
- Same header as the rest of the paper: `Last name + page #` top-right.

**The MLA 9 template — nine "core elements," in this order**, each followed by the punctuation shown:

1. **Author.**
2. **"Title of Source."** *(or* Title *if it's a whole book)*
3. ***Title of Container,*** *(the bigger work it sits in — website, journal, database)*
4. **Other contributors,** *(editor, translator)*
5. **Version,**
6. **Number,** *(vol., no.)*
7. **Publisher,**
8. **Publication date,**
9. **Location.** *(page range, URL, or DOI)*

Skip any element that doesn't apply. **Author = `Last, First.`** Titles of whole works (books, websites, journals) are *italicized*; titles of parts (articles, web pages, chapters) go in **"quotation marks."**

### The three you'll actually need

**A. BOOK** (one author):
```
Jensen, Frances E. The Teenage Brain: A Neuroscientist's Survival Guide.
     HarperCollins, 2015.
```
> Pattern: `Last, First. Title of Book. Publisher, Year.`

**B. WEBSITE / web page** (article on a site, with author):
```
Carter, Lauren. "Why Teenagers Don't Get Enough Sleep." National Sleep
     Foundation, 14 Mar. 2023, www.sleepfoundation.org/teens-and-sleep.
```
> Pattern: `Last, First. "Page Title." Website Name, Day Mon. Year, URL.`
> Drop the `https://`. If there's no author, start with the "Page Title." If there's no date, write `n.d.` Months abbreviate (Jan., Feb., Mar., Apr., …) — but spell out May, June, July.

**C. DATABASE article** (journal article you found through a library database — this is the one that trips everyone up because it has **two containers**: the journal *and* the database):
```
Okafor, Daniel. "Adolescent Sleep Deprivation and Academic Performance."
     Journal of Adolescent Health, vol. 58, no. 2, 2016, pp. 134-141.
     JSTOR, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2015.10.002.
```
> Pattern: `Last, First. "Article Title." Journal Name, vol. #, no. #, Year, pp. ##-##. Database Name, DOI or URL.`
> Use the **DOI** if one exists (write it as `https://doi.org/...`); otherwise the stable URL. Note `pp.` for a page *range*, `p.` for a single page.

## 8. Avoiding plagiarism (and surviving turnitin.com)

turnitin compares your paper to billions of sources and flags overlap. You avoid trouble not by hiding overlap but by **citing honestly**.

**You MUST cite when you:**
- Quote any words directly.
- Paraphrase or summarize someone's idea, data, or argument.
- Use a statistic, study finding, or specific fact that isn't common knowledge.

**You DON'T need to cite:**
- Common knowledge ("WWII ended in 1945," "water is H₂O").
- Your own original analysis and conclusions.

**Plagiarism red flags to self-check before turnitin sees it:**
- A "paraphrase" that's the original with synonyms swapped → **rewrite from scratch or quote it.**
- A quote with no citation → add `(Author #)`.
- An in-text cite with **no matching Works Cited entry** (or vice versa) → every cite needs its twin.
- Forgetting to cite **paraphrased** material because "I put it in my own words." Own words still need the source — the *idea* isn't yours.

**A turnitin score is not a verdict.** Matches on your quoted material (properly in quotes + cited) and your Works Cited are normal and fine. What you're checking for is *unquoted, uncited* matching text. If you cited honestly at the note stage, you're safe.

## Pre-submission checklist (run this before turnitin)

- [ ] Thesis is **arguable** (a smart person could disagree) and names my reasoning.
- [ ] Every body paragraph hits the **2:1 ratio** — more of my commentary than evidence.
- [ ] **Every** quote, stat, and paraphrase has an in-text cite `(Author #)` — no comma, no "p."
- [ ] No **dropped quotes** — every quote is framed (signal phrase) and then analyzed.
- [ ] Sources passed the **CRAAP test**; no Wikipedia/blogs/AI as cited sources.
- [ ] I have the **required number and mix** of sources (book / database / website per the assignment).
- [ ] **Every in-text cite has a matching Works Cited entry**, and every entry is cited at least once.
- [ ] Works Cited: own page, `Works Cited` centered (no bold/italics), **double-spaced**, **hanging indent**, **alphabetical**.
- [ ] Titles correct: books/sites/journals *italic*; articles/pages "in quotes."
- [ ] Whole paper: double-spaced, 12pt readable font, 1" margins, last-name+page header.
- [ ] Submitted to **turnitin.com** and I reviewed the originality report for *uncited* matches.

## Practice problems (cover the answers first)

### Practice 1 — Format a BOOK as a Works Cited entry
You used this book:
- Title: *Why We Sleep: Unlocking the Power of Sleep and Dreams*
- Author: Matthew Walker
- Publisher: Scribner
- Year: 2017

**Write the MLA 9 Works Cited entry.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
Walker, Matthew. Why We Sleep: Unlocking the Power of Sleep and Dreams.
     Scribner, 2017.
```
Pattern: `Last, First. Title (italicized). Publisher, Year.` Hanging indent on the second line; period at the end.
</details>

### Practice 2 — Format a DATABASE article + write its in-text citation
You found this through JSTOR:
- Article: "Start Times, Sleep, and Student Achievement"
- Author: Maria Lopez
- Journal: *Educational Researcher*
- Volume 49, Issue 3, year 2020, pages 188–199
- DOI: 10.3102/0013189X20912345
- You quote it on its page 191.

**(a) Write the Works Cited entry. (b) Write the in-text citation for the quote.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Works Cited:**
```
Lopez, Maria. "Start Times, Sleep, and Student Achievement." Educational
     Researcher, vol. 49, no. 3, 2020, pp. 188-199. JSTOR,
     https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X20912345.
```
Article title in "quotes," journal *italicized*, `vol.`/`no.`, page *range* = `pp.`, then the database (*JSTOR*, italicized) and the DOI as a `https://doi.org/` link.

**(b) In-text:** `(Lopez 191)` — author last name + page, no comma, no "p." If you name her in the sentence ("Lopez found that…"), use just `(191)`.
</details>

### Practice 3 — Fix the plagiarism / citation errors
Here's a student sentence. **Find and fix every problem:**

> Teenagers need about nine hours of sleep but most get far less. (Walker, pg. 14) This is a major issue.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Three problems:

1. **Wrong parenthetical format.** MLA is `(Walker 14)` — **no comma**, **no "pg."**, and no period *inside* before it.
2. **The citation is outside the sentence's period.** The cite belongs *inside* the sentence it supports, with the period *after* it: `... far less (Walker 14).`
3. **Dropped fact + no analysis.** "This is a major issue" is filler, not commentary — it breaks the 2:1 ratio. Frame the source and analyze it.

**Fixed version:**
> Sleep researcher Matthew Walker reports that teenagers need roughly nine hours of sleep yet most fall well short of that (14). This chronic deficit matters because the teenage years are exactly when the brain is consolidating learning, so lost sleep doesn't just cause fatigue — it directly undercuts the academic performance schools are trying to improve.
</details>
