# The A-Essay Guide

### Analytical literary-analysis writing, built EXACTLY on Mrs. Pagtakhan's rubric

> **Why this matters for your grade:** In Mrs. Pagtakhan's class, the analytical essays are the single biggest grade driver — they show up on *Julius Caesar*, *Night*, *The Book Thief*, the MLA research essay, *The Kite Runner*, and *Life of Pi*, so the same rubric earns (or costs) you points all year long. Master this one format and you stop losing easy points on RIP words, summary topic sentences, and dropped quotes — which is exactly the gap between a B-essay and an A-essay.

**How to use this guide:** Read a section, then *do the drill at the bottom that matches it*. Don't just read — fix the broken examples yourself, then check the answer. The practice is where the grade comes from.

---

## The big picture: what an A-essay looks like

An analytical essay proves a **thematic argument** about a text using **embedded quotes** and **commentary**. The shape is an hourglass:

```
        BROAD theme idea (no text mentioned)
         \                              /
          \   INTRO narrows to THESIS  /
           \                          /
            ====  BODY PARAGRAPHS  ====   <- specific: characters, scenes, quotes
           /                          \
          /  CONCLUSION widens back out \
         /                              \
        BROAD closing thought
```

Three rules run through *everything*:

1. **Theme, not plot.** You are proving an idea about life that the text reveals — not retelling what happened.
2. **Commentary outweighs evidence ~2:1.** For every line of quote, write about two lines explaining how it proves your thesis.
3. **No RIP words, no personal pronouns.** These are automatic point losses. Editing for them is the cheapest grade boost you will ever get.

## Part A — Each part of the essay (GOOD vs BAD)

### 1. The Introduction (the triangle: broad → specific)

**The rules:**
- The **first sentence must NOT mention the text in any way** — no title, no author, no character name. It is a **broad statement about a THEME**.
- A *theme* is an idea about life (courage, guilt, identity, the cost of survival). It is **NOT a tool** — symbolism, colors, imagery, and "the author" are not themes.
- Mention the **title + author** *before* you reference any content from the text.
- The **THESIS is the LAST sentence** of the intro.

**BAD intro opener:**
> In *The Book Thief* by Markus Zusak, the author uses symbolism and colors to show a lot about World War II.

*Why it fails:* It names the title, author, AND a "tool" (symbolism/colors) in sentence one. "A lot" is a RIP phrase. "The author uses..." is the banned tool-based opener.

**GOOD intro opener (broad theme, no text):**
> Words carry a power that can either destroy a person or save one. During the darkest moments of history, language becomes both a weapon and a refuge.

*Why it works:* Broad idea about life (the power of words). No title, author, or character. It sets up a theme the essay will prove.

**GOOD full intro (narrowing to a thesis):**
> Words carry a power that can either destroy a person or save one. Throughout history, the same language that spreads hatred can also offer shelter to the powerless. In *The Book Thief*, Markus Zusak follows a young girl in Nazi Germany who learns to read in the middle of a regime built on propaganda. As Liesel steals books and shares them, words shift from a tool of fear into a means of survival. Zusak ultimately argues that language gains its meaning from the person who wields it, transforming ordinary words into instruments of either cruelty or compassion.

*Notice:* sentence 1 = broad theme; title + author appear *before* any plot detail; the **last sentence is the thesis**.

---

### 2. The Thesis (last sentence of the intro)

**The rule:** A clear, specific, **arguable THEMATIC argument** that goes BEYOND summary. Ask yourself: **"What THEME am I proving, and WHY is it true?"**

**BAD thesis (summary):**
> In *The Book Thief*, Liesel steals books and reads them to her neighbors during the war.

*Why it fails:* That's a plot summary. Nobody could disagree with it. There's no theme and no "why."

**BAD thesis (too vague):**
> *The Book Thief* shows that words are important.

*Why it fails:* "Important" how? Not arguable, not specific.

**GOOD thesis (thematic + arguable + "why"):**
> Zusak argues that language gains its meaning from the person who wields it, transforming ordinary words into instruments of either cruelty or compassion.

*Why it works:* It names a **theme** (the power of words depends on the user), it is **arguable** (someone could claim words have fixed meaning), and it explains **why** the theme is true.

**Thesis formula you can reuse:**
> [Author] argues that [theme/claim about life] because/by [the "why" — how the text proves it].

---

### 3. Topic Sentences (first sentence of each body paragraph)

**The rules:**
- Mention the text **specifically** — a **character or a scene**, NOT by title/author.
- Tie **directly to the thesis**.
- Contain **commentary, not summary**.
- Contain **NO quotes**.

**BAD topic sentence (summary + title):**
> In *The Book Thief*, Liesel paints words on the basement wall with Max.

*Why it fails:* Names the title (banned in topic sentences), and it's pure plot summary — no argument.

**GOOD topic sentence (specific character + commentary tied to thesis):**
> Liesel's growing command of language reveals that words become powerful only when a person chooses to use them for connection rather than control.

*Why it works:* Specific character (Liesel), no title/author, no quote, and it advances the thesis with **commentary** (words = power through human choice).

---

### 4. The Body Paragraph (the engine of the essay)

**The required structure, in order:**

```
1. Topic Sentence            (commentary, no quote, ties to thesis)
2. Clarification / lead-in   (sets up the first quote in your own words)
3. Quote 1 (embedded) + analysis
4. Quote 2 (embedded) + analysis
   -> >= 2 quotes per paragraph
   -> >= 3 quotes per paragraph if the essay has only TWO body paragraphs
5. Final insight             (the deepest "so what?" of the paragraph)
6. Concluding sentence       (restate topic sentence in new words; mention text; NO quote; do NOT transition)
```

Keep the ratio roughly **2 parts commentary : 1 part evidence**.

**GOOD body paragraph (annotated):**

> *(Topic sentence)* Liesel's growing command of language reveals that words become powerful only when a person chooses to use them for connection rather than control. *(Lead-in)* Early on, she cannot read at all, and her hunger to learn turns letters into something she physically craves. *(Quote 1 embedded + analysis)* When she describes the alphabet as letters she could "see them somehow," Liesel treats reading less as a school skill than as a form of survival, a way to grasp a world that has stripped away her family. The verb "see" suggests that literacy gives her vision in a place engineered to keep people blind to the truth. *(Quote 2 embedded + analysis)* Later, when she writes that words were "fed to her, in the early hours," the image of feeding ties language directly to nourishment, implying that without it she would starve emotionally. Mining the word "fed" shows that, for Liesel, language is not decoration but sustenance. *(Final insight)* Her transformation from an illiterate girl into a writer proves that the same words used by the regime to spread fear can, in different hands, keep a person alive. *(Concluding sentence)* In short, the girl who once could not read becomes living evidence that words draw their force from the heart that chooses them.

*Why it works:* Two embedded quotes, ~2:1 commentary, re-quotes single words ("see," "fed") in the analysis, a final insight, and a concluding sentence that restates the topic sentence without quoting or transitioning.

---

### 5. Commentary — the "Black Out the Quote" test

This is the most important skill, and the easiest place to lose points.

**The test:** If you **blacked out the quote**, the reader should *still* know you are analyzing **THIS specific text**. Commentary must tie directly to the quote (even re-quoting a single word) **and** to the thesis. **Never generic.**

**BAD commentary (generic — fails the blackout test):**
> When Liesel reads, the author writes that words were "fed to her, in the early hours." This shows that reading is really important and that words can be powerful.

*Why it fails:* "This shows" is a RIP phrase. "Reading is really important" is generic — black out the quote and this could describe *any* book. "Really" is a RIP word.

**GOOD commentary (specific — passes the blackout test):**
> Describing language as something "fed to her, in the early hours," Zusak frames Liesel's literacy as nourishment rather than schooling. The word "fed" casts books as food in a household where food is scarce, suggesting that words sustain Liesel when bread cannot. In a Germany that starves its people of truth, her private hunger for reading becomes a quiet act of resistance.

*Why it works:* Black out the quote and you *still* know this is Liesel, *The Book Thief*, and the thesis about words-as-power. It re-quotes "fed," ties to the thesis, and uses zero RIP words.

---

### 6. Quotes — how to embed them

**The rules:**
- **Blend the quote grammatically into your own sentence** — do NOT drop it in after a comma or colon.
- **Splice it SHORT** — quote only the words you'll analyze.
- **At least 2 quotes per body paragraph** (3 if only two body paragraphs).
- **MLA citation** in parentheses after the quote; **the period goes after the citation**.
- **Commas and periods go INSIDE the quotation marks** (American usage) — *except* when a parenthetical citation follows, in which case the period moves to after the parenthesis.

**BAD (dropped quote after a colon):**
> Liesel loved words: "She read it. She read it again" (Zusak 38).

*Why it fails:* The quote is dumped in after a colon, not woven into a sentence. The quote is also longer than it needs to be.

**BAD (dropped after a comma):**
> Max sees the danger in language, "The words. Why did they have to exist?" (Zusak 521).

*Why it fails:* Comma-splice drop-in; the quote isn't part of the grammar of your sentence.

**GOOD (blended + spliced short):**
> Liesel returns to the page so compulsively that she "read it again" (Zusak 38), turning a single book into a refuge she can re-enter at will.

> Reflecting on Hitler's propaganda, Max wonders why such words "have to exist" (Zusak 521), exposing his fear that language itself can be weaponized.

*Why it works:* The quote is part of the sentence's grammar, only the analyzed words are quoted, and MLA punctuation is correct (period **after** the parenthetical citation).

**Quick MLA reminders (already standard, matches the handout):**
- Short prose quote: blend it in, then `(Author Page)` — e.g., `(Zusak 38)`. No "p." or "pg."
- If you already named the author in the sentence, the citation can be just the page: `(38)`.
- Title of a novel/play is *italicized*: *The Book Thief*, *Julius Caesar*, *Night*, *The Kite Runner*, *Life of Pi*.
- **Comma/period inside the closing quotation mark** when there is no citation: `Liesel calls them "stolen words," and treasures them.`
- When a citation follows, the closing punctuation goes **after** the parenthesis: `Liesel treasures the "stolen words" (Zusak 41).`

---

### 7. The Concluding Sentence (of each body paragraph)

**The rules:**
- **Restate the topic sentence in different words.**
- **Mention the text** (character/scene).
- **NO quote.**
- **Do NOT transition** to the next paragraph.

**BAD concluding sentence:**
> This shows words are powerful, and next Liesel will show even more bravery when she reads in the bomb shelter.

*Why it fails:* "This shows" is a RIP phrase, and it **transitions** to the next paragraph (banned). It also doesn't restate the topic sentence.

**GOOD concluding sentence:**
> The girl who once could not read becomes living proof that words draw their force from the heart that chooses them.

*Why it works:* Restates the topic sentence in fresh words, mentions the character, no quote, no transition.

---

### 8. The Conclusion paragraph (inverted triangle: specific → broad)

**The rules:**
- **Never** write "In conclusion."
- **Restate the thesis in new words and mention the text in the FIRST sentence.**
- **End broad** (inverted triangle), leaving the reader with a thought.

**BAD conclusion:**
> In conclusion, *The Book Thief* shows that words are really powerful, like I said in my thesis.

*Why it fails:* "In conclusion" is banned. "Really" is a RIP word. "I" / "my" are banned personal pronouns. It restates nothing in new words.

**GOOD conclusion:**
> In Nazi Germany, a young girl proves that the value of a word lives not in the word itself but in the hands that offer it. Across Liesel's stolen books and Max's whispered stories, language becomes either a chain or a lifeline depending on who holds it. Long after the bombs fall, the question remains for every generation: when given the power of words, will people choose to wound or to heal?

*Why it works:* First sentence restates the thesis in new language and references the text; the final sentence zooms out to a universal question (the inverted triangle).

## Part B — The RIP Words list (and why it matters)

**Why editing for these matters:** Each RIP word costs about **2 points**, and **"you" / second person costs -5**. A single careless paragraph can quietly drop an A to a C *before* the content is even graded. A five-minute "RIP-word sweep" at the end is the highest-value edit you can make.

**The rules:** RIP words are **banned except inside a quotation**. **NO slang, ever.** **NO personal pronouns** (I, we, me, my, us, our) in formal analytical essays.

| RIP word / phrase | Fix it with |
|---|---|
| you / your / you're (**−5, 2nd person**) | (remove entirely — never address the reader) |
| a lot | many, numerous, considerable |
| 'cause | because |
| bad | harmful, cruel, destructive |
| big | large, significant, vast |
| etc. | (name the items, or "and similar") |
| fun | enjoyable, engaging |
| in conclusion | (just start the sentence) |
| in my opinion | (state the claim directly) |
| kind of / sort of | somewhat, partially |
| lots | many |
| nice | kind, pleasant, generous |
| ok / okay | acceptable, adequate |
| pretty (as in "pretty good") | fairly, quite, rather |
| really | (delete, or "genuinely / deeply") |
| stuff | items, details, elements |
| this shows / this quote shows / this quote | "Zusak reveals…", "The image suggests…", "The word ___ implies…" |
| 'til | until |
| very | (delete, or use a stronger word: not "very sad" → "devastated") |
| get / got / getting / gets | obtain, receive, become, understand |

**Banned personal pronouns:** I, we, me, my, us, our.
> **One exception in the whole year:** the *Kite Runner* **One-Pager** personal response allows **1st person** — but **never 2nd person ("you")** even there.

**Before / after:**
> BAD: *In my opinion, you can really see that Liesel got a lot of stuff out of reading, which is pretty nice.*
> GOOD: *Liesel draws considerable strength from reading, a quiet refuge in a brutal world.*

## Part C — The process: brainstorm → outline → draft → edit

### Step 1 — Brainstorm (gather more than you need)
- Pick your **theme** first, then your **thesis**.
- For **each** topic-sentence idea, find **at least 4 quotes**. You'll cut down to the best 2–3 per paragraph.
- Write the **page number** next to every quote *now* so MLA citations are painless later.

### Step 2 — Outline (the guide-word template)
Fill this in. (For **in-class** outlines, only the **thesis, topic sentences, and quotes** must be complete sentences — the rest can be notes.)

```
INTRO
  Broad theme hook (NO text): ____________________________________
  Title + author + 1-line context: _______________________________
  THESIS (last sentence): ________________________________________

BODY 1
  Topic sentence (character/scene, no title, no quote, ties to thesis): ____
  Lead-in: _______________________________________________________
  Quote 1 (short) + page: "____________________" (Author ##)
    Analysis (re-quote a word; tie to thesis): __________________
  Quote 2 (short) + page: "____________________" (Author ##)
    Analysis: ______________________________________________________
  Final insight (so what?): ______________________________________
  Concluding sentence (restate TS, mention text, no quote, no transition): __

BODY 2  (repeat structure)
  ...

BODY 3  (optional; if you have only 2 body paras total, each needs >=3 quotes)
  ...

CONCLUSION
  First sentence: restate thesis in new words + mention text: _____
  Widen out to a broad closing thought (leave a question/idea): ___
```

### Step 3 — Draft
- **Handwritten draft first** → take a **photo** (per her process).
- Then **type it**.
- **Read it ALOUD** — fluency is graded above all. If you stumble, the sentence needs fixing (usually wordiness or passive voice).

### Step 4 — Edit
- Run the **checklist below**.
- Do a dedicated **RIP-word sweep** (Ctrl/Cmd-F each banned word).
- Submit to **turnitin.com** before the deadline. **No late work** — budget a day of buffer.

> Do each drill on paper or in your head **before** looking at the answer. The fixing is the learning.

### Drill 1 — Fix the intro opener
The first sentence breaks the rule. Rewrite it so it opens broad with a theme and mentions no text.

> *In* Night *by Elie Wiesel, the author uses imagery of fire and darkness to show a lot about the Holocaust.*

<details><summary><b>Answer</b></summary>

Two problems: it names title + author + a "tool" (imagery) in sentence one, and "a lot" is a RIP phrase. Open broad on a theme instead:

> **Survival can demand a price so steep that the survivor is no longer the same person who began the struggle.** *(Then, in a later sentence: "In* Night*, Elie Wiesel recounts…")*

</details>

---

### Drill 2 — Turn a summary thesis into an analytical one
Rewrite as an arguable thematic claim with a "why."

> *In* Julius Caesar*, Brutus joins the conspiracy and helps kill Caesar.*

<details><summary><b>Answer</b></summary>

That's plot. Make it thematic, arguable, and add a "why":

> **Shakespeare argues that good intentions cannot purify a violent act, because Brutus's honorable motives still lead Rome into the very chaos he hoped to prevent.**

</details>

---

### Drill 3 — Fix the summary topic sentence
Make it analytical, name a character/scene, drop the title, and add no quote.

> *In* The Book Thief*, Death narrates the story and collects souls during the war.*

<details><summary><b>Answer</b></summary>

> **Death's weary, compassionate narration reveals that even an inhuman force can be worn down by the cruelty humans inflict on one another.**

(Specific narrator-as-character, no title, no quote, commentary that ties to a theme.)

</details>

---

### Drill 4 — Embed the dropped quote
Blend this into your own sentence and splice it short. (Quote: "I am no orator, as Brutus is" — Antony, *Julius Caesar*.)

> *Antony is sneaky in his speech: "I am no orator, as Brutus is" (Shakespeare 3.2.221).*

<details><summary><b>Answer</b></summary>

"Sneaky" is too casual, and the quote is dropped after a colon. Blend and splice:

> **By insisting that he is "no orator" (Shakespeare 3.2.221), Antony disguises a masterful manipulation as plain, honest speech.**

(Grammatically blended, spliced to the key words, MLA period after the citation.)

</details>

### Drill 5 — Fix the weak commentary (blackout test)
Rewrite so that, if the quote were hidden, the reader still knows this is *Night* and the thesis. (Thesis: the Holocaust strips away faith.)

> *Eliezer says "For the first time, I felt anger rising within me" (Wiesel 33). This quote shows that he is really mad and that things are bad.*

<details><summary><b>Answer</b></summary>

RIP words ("this quote shows," "really," "bad") and totally generic. Fix:

> **When Eliezer feels "anger rising within" him (Wiesel 33), Wiesel marks the first crack in the boy's once-unquestioning faith. The verb "rising" suggests a force he cannot control, foreshadowing how the camps will slowly burn away the devotion that had defined his identity.**

(Black out the quote and you still know it's Eliezer, *Night*, and the loss-of-faith thesis.)

</details>

### Drill 6 — RIP-word sweep
Rewrite this sentence with zero RIP words and no personal pronouns.

> *In my opinion, you can really tell that Amir got a lot of guilt, and it's pretty bad how he treats Hassan.*

<details><summary><b>Answer</b></summary>

Kills: "in my opinion," "you," "really," "got," "a lot," "pretty," "bad."

### Drill 7 — Fix the concluding sentence (of a body paragraph)
Remove the transition and the RIP phrase; restate the topic sentence; mention the text; no quote.
(Topic sentence was: *Pi's reliance on ritual reveals that faith is what keeps the mind intact under impossible conditions.*)

> *This shows that faith is important, and next Pi will use storytelling to survive too.*

<details><summary><b>Answer</b></summary>

> **By clinging to the rhythms of prayer at sea, Pi proves that faith functions less as belief than as the scaffolding that holds a breaking mind together.**

(No "this shows," no transition to the next paragraph, restates the TS, references Pi/the text, no quote.)

</details>

### Drill 9 (Challenge) — Build a full body paragraph from parts
You're given a topic sentence and two short quotes. Assemble a full paragraph in the required order (TS → lead-in → Q1+analysis → Q2+analysis → final insight → concluding sentence), 2:1 commentary, no RIP words.

- **Topic sentence:** *Hassan's loyalty exposes Amir's cowardice as the true wound of the novel.*
- Quote 1: Hassan says, "For you, a thousand times over" (Hosseini 67).
- Quote 2: Amir admits he "ran" because he "was a coward" (Hosseini 77).

<details><summary><b>Model answer</b></summary>

> Hassan's loyalty exposes Amir's cowardice as the true wound of the novel. Even as a servant treated as lesser, Hassan offers a devotion Amir cannot bring himself to return. When Hassan promises to chase the kite "a thousand times over" (Hosseini 67), the vow measures love in endless repetition, a constancy that makes Amir's later silence all the more damning. Mining the phrase "thousand times over" reveals a friendship Hassan treats as limitless, even as Amir rations his own loyalty down to nothing. That gap widens when Amir confesses he "ran" because he "was a coward" (Hosseini 77); the blunt verb strips away every excuse and names the betrayal for what it is. The two boys stand as mirror images — one who gives everything and one who flees — and the contrast proves that the deepest cruelty is not the assault Hassan suffers but the friend who chooses to look away. In the end, Hassan's boundless devotion becomes the measure against which Amir's failure is judged for the rest of his life.

</details>

### Drill 10 (Challenge) — Spot every error
Find all the rubric violations in this paragraph, then rewrite the topic + concluding sentences correctly.

> *In* Life of Pi*, Pi is on a boat with a tiger. The author uses symbolism to show survival. "I had to tame him" (Martel 164), this quote shows Pi is brave. You can really see he got tougher. In conclusion, the tiger is a big symbol.*

<details><summary><b>Answer — errors</b></summary>

1. Topic sentence names the **title** and is **plot summary**.
2. "The author uses symbolism" — symbolism is a **tool**, not a theme.
3. Quote is **dropped** after a comma, not blended.
4. **"This quote shows"** — RIP phrase.
5. **"brave"** is fine, but the commentary is **generic** (fails blackout test).
6. **"You"** (−5), **"really," "got," "big"** — RIP words / 2nd person.
7. **"In conclusion"** — banned.
8. Concluding line calls the tiger a "**big symbol**" — tool-based, not thematic, and uses a RIP word.

**Fixed topic sentence:**
> Pi's struggle to coexist with Richard Parker reveals that survival depends on imposing order onto a world that has none.

**Fixed concluding sentence:**
> By learning to share his lifeboat with a predator, Pi shows that endurance is less about conquering fear than about giving it a shape one can live beside.

</details>

## One-page "do this every time" recap

1. **Open broad** (theme, no text) → **end the intro on the thesis** (arguable, thematic, "why").
2. **Topic sentence** = character/scene + commentary, **no title, no quote**, tied to thesis.
3. **Body** = TS → lead-in → Q1+analysis → Q2+analysis → insight → concluding sentence. **≥2 quotes** (≥3 if only two body paras). **2:1 commentary**.
4. **Embed quotes**, splice short, MLA `(Author ##)`, period after citation.
5. **Blackout test** every piece of commentary.
6. **Concluding sentence**: restate TS, mention text, no quote, no transition.
7. **Conclusion**: no "In conclusion"; restate thesis + text in sentence one; **end broad**.
8. **RIP-word sweep** + **no personal pronouns** + **no "you"** + **no slang**.
9. **Read aloud** for fluency; cut passive voice and wordiness.
10. **Turnitin before the deadline.** No late work.
