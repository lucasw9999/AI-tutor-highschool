# SAT Vocabulary System — 24 Weeks, Weekly Quizzes + 2 Finals

**Why this matters for your grade:** In Mrs. Pagtakhan's class the weekly SAT vocab quizzes and the two semester Vocab Finals (weeks 1–10, then weeks 1–24) are recurring, near-automatic points — the kind of grade you fully control if you just run a system. Nail 10 words a week with the method below and you bank easy A's *and* the bigger words start showing up in your literary-analysis essays (the real grade driver), which makes those score higher too.

> **How to use this file:** This guide teaches the *method* and *models it* on Week-1 words. Your actual weekly word lists live in the class docs / Mrs. Pagtakhan's handouts. Copy the per-week routine below for every list. Don't try to read this all at once — read Part 1 once, then live in Parts 2–4.

---

## Part 0 — The 60-second overview (read this first)

You have **10 new words every week for 24 weeks** = 240 words by the final. The trap is "cramming the night before the quiz." It works for Friday and fails on the cumulative final, because crammed words evaporate in ~48 hours.

The fix is four moves, in order of power:

1. **Spaced repetition** — review each word on an expanding schedule (today, tomorrow, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks) instead of all at once. This is the single biggest lever.
2. **Word-in-a-sentence** — you don't *know* a word until you can use it in a sentence YOU wrote. Recognition ≠ recall.
3. **Roots / word families** — learn the Latin/Greek part once, unlock 5–10 words for free, and survive words you've never seen on the final.
4. **Mnemonics** — a goofy mental image as a backup "hook" for the 2–3 words that refuse to stick.

Spaced repetition does 70% of the work. The other three exist to make each repetition *stickier*. The rest of this guide is just how to execute these four, fast.

## Part 1 — The four methods, explained fast

### Method 1: Spaced repetition (your #1 tool)

Your memory follows a "forgetting curve" — you forget most new info within a day or two unless you reload it. Each time you successfully recall a word *right before you'd forget it*, the memory gets stronger and the next review can wait longer. So you review on an **expanding schedule**:

| Review | When | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1st pass | Day you get the list (Mon) | Encode |
| 2nd pass | Next day (Tue) | Catch early forgetting |
| 3rd pass | +2 days (Thu) | Strengthen |
| Quiz | Fri | First "real" test |
| 4th pass | The next week | Move toward long-term memory |
| Final pass | ~2 weeks later, then monthly | Lock it for the cumulative final |

**Two ways to run it (pick one):**

- **App (least effort, recommended):** Anki or Quizlet. Make a digital deck; the app schedules reviews for you. In Anki, when you see a card you rate it Again/Hard/Good/Easy and it picks the next date automatically. Quizlet's "Learn" mode does a lighter version.
- **Paper (no app):** Index cards in 5 envelopes/sections labeled *Daily, Every-other-day, Weekly, Bi-weekly, Monthly*. A card you get right moves to the next slower box; a card you miss goes back to *Daily*. This is the "Leitner box."

> **Rule:** Always quiz yourself **definition-from-word AND word-from-definition** (both directions). The quiz will ask you to recognize meaning; your essays need you to *produce* the word. Train both.

### Method 2: Word-in-a-sentence (the recall test)

For every word, write **one original sentence** where the context *proves* you know the meaning. "The man was sycophantic" proves nothing (you could swap any adjective). "The intern laughed at every one of the boss's jokes, even the bad ones — a total **sycophant**" proves it.

Checklist for a good sentence:
- It contains a **context clue** that points to the meaning.
- It's about *your* life, a show, a game, history class — anything memorable beats a generic sentence.
- It uses the **correct part of speech** (noun vs. adjective vs. verb — see each word's tag below).

### Method 3: Roots / word families (the multiplier + safety net)

Most SAT words are built from Latin/Greek parts. Learn the part once and you can *attack a word you've never seen* on the final. Example: `per-` ("completely/thoroughly") + `nec/nic` ("death") → **pernicious** = "deadly," and the same `nec/nic` shows up in *necrosis, internecine, innocuous*. A few high-value roots you'll meet across the year:

| Root/Affix | Meaning | Words it unlocks |
|---|---|---|
| `bene-` / `mal-` | good / bad | benevolent, malevolent, malign |
| `loqu-` / `dict-` | speak / say | loquacious, eloquent, diction, malediction |
| `-cred-` | believe | credible, incredulous, credulity |
| `omni-` / `ubi-` | all / everywhere | omnipresent, **ubiquitous** |
| `in-` / `im-` | not | **implacable**, intractable, impervious |
| `nec-/nic-` | death | **pernicious**, internecine |

> Keep a running "Roots sheet" in your notes. Add a root the first time it appears; by spring you'll have 30–40 and the final gets dramatically easier.

### Method 4: Mnemonics (the backup hook)

A mnemonic is a silly link between the word's *sound* and its *meaning*. Use it only for words that won't stick after a few reviews — don't make one for all 10. The weirder/funnier the image, the better it sticks. Example: **lugubrious** ("mournful") → "a **lugubrious** person looks like a sad, slow slug — *lug-*ging itself along, crying." Picture the crying slug. Done.

## Part 2 — Your reusable per-week routine (copy this for every list)

This is the whole job: about **15 minutes Monday + 5 minutes a day**. Same steps every week, any 10 words.

**MONDAY — Build (15 min)**
1. Get the week's 10 words from the class doc.
2. For each word, write a 4-column card/row: **word | part of speech | one-line definition (in your own words) | original sentence**.
3. Tag any **root** you recognize (add new ones to your Roots sheet).
4. Flag the 2–3 hardest words and write a **mnemonic** only for those.
5. Add all 10 to your Anki/Quizlet deck (or index-card box).

**TUESDAY (3–5 min)** — Review all 10 *word → definition*. Star misses.

**WEDNESDAY (3–5 min)** — Review *definition → word* (the harder direction). Re-read your sentences.

**THURSDAY (5 min)** — Self-quiz: cover the definitions and write them from memory; then cover the words and produce them from the definitions. Re-drill only the misses.

**FRIDAY — Quiz day.** Quick 3-min skim of starred words right before class. Take the quiz.

**SATURDAY/SUNDAY (2 min)** — Move this week's mastered cards to the "Weekly/Bi-weekly" pile so they cycle back later. **This weekend step is what makes the cumulative final easy** — it's the only thing most students skip.

> **Use the words for real:** When you write your next analytical essay or speak in a Socratic/fishbowl, deliberately drop in 1–2 vocab words *correctly*. Production in a real context is the strongest possible review, and it makes your writing sound more sophisticated to Mrs. Pagtakhan.

## Part 3 — Full worked model on the Week-1 words

Here is the method run **end to end** on the 10 sample words, so you can copy the format exactly. (Definitions are in plain English; parts of speech and roots verified.)

| # | Word (part of speech) | Plain-English meaning | Root / why | Mnemonic | Original sentence (with context clue) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **sycophant** (n.) | a suck-up; someone who flatters powerful people to get ahead | Greek *sykon* "fig" + *phainein* "to show" → originally a slanderer/toady | "**Sick-o-fan**" — a sick-obsessed *fan* who praises the star no matter what | The new kid agreed with every single thing the captain said, laughing at jokes that weren't funny — a complete **sycophant**. |
| 2 | **lugubrious** (adj.) | exaggeratedly mournful, gloomy, sad | Latin *lugere* "to mourn" | A crying **slug** *lug*-ging itself along, sobbing | His **lugubrious** tone at the pep rally — slow, moaning, near tears — made the whole gym feel like a funeral. |
| 3 | **pernicious** (adj.) | causing harm in a gradual, hidden, deadly way | Latin *per-* "completely" + *nex/necis* "death" | "*per*-vicious" = thoroughly vicious | The rumor seemed harmless at first, but it spread quietly and ruined her reputation — a truly **pernicious** lie. |
| 4 | **insouciant** (adj.) | carefree; casually unconcerned, can't-be-bothered | French *in-* "not" + *soucier* "to worry" | "**in-SOO-see**" → not seeing/caring about any problem | While everyone panicked about the deadline, he leaned back, headphones on, completely **insouciant**. |
| 5 | **ubiquitous** (adj.) | seeming to be everywhere at once | Latin *ubique* "everywhere" (root `ubi-`) | "**You-bik**" → *you* see it on every *bike*, phone, and billboard | Phone chargers are so **ubiquitous** now that you can find one in every room of the house. |
| 6 | **non sequitur** (n.) | a statement that doesn't logically follow what came before | Latin, literally "it does not follow" | It "does *not* sequence" with the last thing said | When I asked about the homework and he replied "I like pizza," it was a total **non sequitur**. |
| 7 | **restive** (adj.) | restless and impatient, especially under control or restraint | Latin *rester* "to remain" → "refusing to move forward" | A horse that *resists* moving = **rest**less + resist | After forty minutes of the substitute reading aloud, the class grew **restive**, fidgeting and whispering. |
| 8 | **diatribe** (n.) | a long, bitter, angry verbal attack | Greek *dia-* "through/away" + *tribein* "to wear away" (a wearing-down rant) | "**Die-a-tribe**" — a tribe screaming a furious rant at you | Instead of one calm correction, the coach launched into a ten-minute **diatribe** about our laziness. |
| 9 | **jingoistic** (adj.) | aggressively, blindly patriotic; eager for war with other countries | from 1878 British song "by jingo, we've got the ships…" | "**jingo**" = jangling war bells; pure flag-waving aggression | The speech was so **jingoistic** that it treated every other nation as an enemy to be crushed. |
| 10 | **implacable** (adj.) | unable to be calmed, satisfied, or changed; relentless | Latin *im-* "not" + *placare* "to appease" (related to *placate*) | "**im-PLACATE**-able" = you *cannot placate* it | No apology worked; her anger was **implacable**, and she refused to speak to him for the rest of the year. |

> **Notice the moves in that table:** every row has (1) plain meaning, (2) a root or origin so it generalizes, (3) a mnemonic only where the word is tricky, and (4) a sentence with a built-in context clue. That's the whole method in one grid. Reproduce this grid for every weekly list.

### Quick self-quiz on Week 1 (10 items) — answers below, no peeking

**A. Match / recall (write the word):**
1. A long, bitter, angry rant against something. → _______
2. Seeming to be everywhere at once. → _______
3. A suck-up who flatters powerful people. → _______
4. Carefree and casually unconcerned. → _______
5. Impossible to calm down or satisfy. → _______

**B. Fill in the blank with the best Week-1 word:**
6. The horses grew ______ in the thunderstorm, stamping and refusing to stand still.
7. Saying "It's raining, so I should buy new sneakers" is a ______ — the conclusion doesn't follow.
8. The slow-acting poison was ______, killing the patient over many weeks without obvious symptoms.
9. His ______ expression at the memorial — head bowed, eyes wet — matched the somber mood.
10. The politician's ______ slogans painted every neighboring country as a threat to be conquered.

**Answers:**
1. diatribe · 2. ubiquitous · 3. sycophant · 4. insouciant · 5. implacable · 6. restive · 7. non sequitur · 8. pernicious · 9. lugubrious · 10. jingoistic

> Score it: **8–10** = ready for Friday, just keep spaced reviews going. **5–7** = re-drill the misses Wed/Thu. **<5** = your Monday sentences/mnemonics are too generic; rewrite them about your own life.

## Part 4 — Prepping for the two cumulative Vocab Finals

**Final 1 covers weeks 1–10 (~100 words). Final 2 covers weeks 1–24 (~240 words).** Cramming 100–240 words the night before is impossible — but if you did the weekend "move it to the slower pile" step all along, the words are *already* in long-term memory and you're just doing a warm-up. The plan:

**4 weeks out — Diagnose.**
- Run one big self-test over **all** weeks (Anki/Quizlet will quiz the whole deck; on paper, shuffle every box and run through once).
- Make a single **"Leaky List"** of every word you miss. This is now your only study material — ignore the ~80% you already know.

**3–2 weeks out — Attack the Leaky List.**
- Re-do the Part-3 grid (root + mnemonic + fresh sentence) only for Leaky List words.
- Group leaks by **root family** (all the `mal-` words together, all the "not"/`in-/im-` words together). Words learned in families stick better and the final's unfamiliar words become guessable from their parts.
- Re-quiz the Leaky List every other day; remove a word the moment you get it right twice in a row.

**1 week out — Mixed, both directions, timed.**
- Quiz **definition → word** and **word → definition**, all weeks shuffled, under a light time limit so recall is fast (the final won't give you time to ruminate).
- Do 2–3 "interleaved" mini-tests of 20 random words each rather than one marathon — testing yourself (retrieval practice) beats re-reading every time.

**Night before — Light only.**
- One pass over the (now tiny) Leaky List + sleep. Sleep consolidates memory; a 1 a.m. cram trades a few recognized words for a foggy brain on a 240-word test. Bad trade.

**Day of — 5-min warm-up** on the 10 stubbornest words, then go.

> **The one habit that wins the final:** the 2-minute weekend step in Part 2. If you only remember one thing from this guide, it's that *moving mastered words into a slower review cycle every weekend* turns the cumulative final from a nightmare into a formality.

## Part 5 — Practice problems (with answers)

These test the **method**, using Week-1 words as the material. Answers follow each set.

### Set A — Definitions (write the meaning in your own words)
1. insouciant
2. implacable
3. diatribe
4. restive
5. pernicious

**Answers A:**
1. carefree, casually unconcerned · 2. impossible to calm, satisfy, or appease; relentless · 3. a long, bitter, angry verbal attack · 4. restless/impatient, especially when held back or restrained · 5. causing harm gradually and in a hidden, deadly way

### Set B — Use it correctly (write an original sentence with a context clue)
For each, your sentence must include a clue that *proves* the meaning, and use the right part of speech.
6. sycophant (noun)
7. ubiquitous (adjective)
8. lugubrious (adjective)

**Sample strong answers B (yours will differ — check that the clue proves the meaning):**
6. "She surrounded herself with **sycophants** who praised every decision and never once disagreed." *(clue: praise + never disagree = flatterer)*
7. "Security cameras have become **ubiquitous** — they're mounted on nearly every street corner, store, and doorbell." *(clue: on nearly every…)*
8. "The eulogy was so **lugubrious**, delivered through tears in a slow, heavy voice, that the whole room began to cry." *(clue: tears, slow, heavy = mournful)*

### Set C — Roots (generalize, the way the final wants)
9. **pernicious** shares the root `nec-/nic-` ("death"). What do you predict **internecine** (describing a conflict) roughly means?
10. **implacable** uses `im-` ("not") + *placare* ("to appease"). What does the related verb **placate** mean?
11. **ubiquitous** comes from `ubi-` ("everywhere"). If a service is described as **ubiquitous computing**, what's the rough idea?

**Answers C:**
9. mutually destructive / deadly to both sides (an internecine conflict is one that kills/ruins everyone involved). · 10. to calm someone down, soothe, or make them less angry (to appease). · 11. computing that's available everywhere / built into everything around you, all the time.

### Set D — Spot the method mistake (why these study habits fail)
12. "I read the list of 10 words five times Thursday night and aced Friday's quiz, so I'm set for the final." — what's the flaw?
13. "My sentence was: 'The teacher was very pernicious.'" — why is this a weak study sentence?
14. "I made a mnemonic and wrote a sentence for all 10 words, every week. It's exhausting and I quit." — what should change?

**Answers D:**
12. Friday cramming builds *short-term* recognition that fades in ~48 hours; with no spaced reviews (and no weekend "move to slower pile" step), almost none of it survives to the cumulative final. Add the expanding-schedule reviews. · 13. There's no context clue — "very [any adjective]" proves nothing about meaning; it tests neither recall nor understanding. Rewrite with a clue ("…spread quietly and ruined her reputation"). · 14. Mnemonics are a *backup* for only the 2–3 stubborn words, not all 10. Doing all four methods maximally for every word causes burnout; spaced repetition + sentences carry most words, and you reserve mnemonics for the holdouts.
